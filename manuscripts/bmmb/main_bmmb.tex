\abstract{The dimensions of the pelvic canal describe unloaded anatomy, but its initial response also depends on available deformation directions and the forces that recruit them. We examined these three levels in 278 computed-tomography-derived pelvic finite-element models with individual geometry and bone elasticity on a shared reference domain. The 15 lowest linearised stiffness modes were assessed by spectral correspondence, basis-invariant diameter capacities, and modal strain-energy fractions under two standing and three comparative expansion loads. Modes 1--3 exchanged labels in fewer than 0.4\% of subjects and dominated standing: mode 2 carried approximately 84.5\% of retained energy in two-leg stance, while modes 1--3 carried approximately 92.0\% in one-leg stance. Internal-ring expansion instead recruited the 9--10 pair (approximately 51\%). Among 152 members of that cluster, median anteroposterior inlet coupling at rank 9 changed from 0.454 to 0.139\,mm/mm between no-exchange and exchange groups, whereas the pair's capacity changed from 0.551 to 0.525\,mm/mm ($q=0.040$). Thus, a large rank-specific change accompanied a smaller, detectable change in available capacity. Ischial distraction recruited the 5--6 pair despite larger interspinous and bituberous capacities in 9--10. In this model of initial elastic response, anatomical variation defines potential directions of canal change, while stiffness and loading determine which of those directions contribute to the response.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
The pelvic ring carries loads from the trunk to the lower limbs while enclosing a canal whose dimensions vary across people. Its response depends on bony geometry and material properties, the sacroiliac and pubic articulations, and ligamentous restraints~\citep{Snijders1993,Vleeming2012}. A canal diameter measured in the unloaded pelvis describes a starting configuration; it does not specify how far that diameter can change for a given displacement, or which change will follow a particular force. These are distinct questions about morphology, kinematic capacity, and loaded response. Resolving their relationship matters when interpreting pelvic variation in locomotor and obstetric contexts, even though neither locomotor efficiency nor birth outcome can be inferred from a diameter alone.

The canal is morphologically diverse within and among human populations~\citep{BettiManica2018}. Its shape covaries with stature and head size~\citep{FischerMitteroecker2015}, and its dimensions change through development and adulthood~\citep{Huseynov2016}. The inlet, midpelvis, and outlet differ in directional geometry and functional setting~\citep{StansfieldCanal2021}. These observations establish anatomical variability, but they do not tell us whether differently shaped pelves retain similar possibilities for elastic diameter change. Magnetic-resonance measurements show that posture can alter bony midpelvic and outlet dimensions~\citep{Reitter2014}. Such positional changes demonstrate mobility in vivo, although they combine joint motion, loading, and posture and cannot identify the intrinsic deformation directions of a pelvic FE model.

Existing biomechanics provides parts of this picture. CT-derived FE models have shown between-pelvis differences in strain under gait configurations~\citep{Salo2017PelvicStrain}, and ligament modelling changes pelvic kinematics~\citep{Henys2022Ligaments}. Musculoskeletal models estimate joint reactions during standing and walking~\citep{Chen2026PelvicModel}. Population models are needed because the response of an average anatomy need not equal the average response~\citep{CookRobertson2016,Fernandez2020Population}. Statistical shape modes describe variation in form~\citep{Arand2019PelvicShape}; stiffness modes instead describe directions in which a specified elastic structure can move. Earlier pelvic modal studies examined model convergence and validation~\citep{HenysCapek2019Modal} and related spectral descriptors to anthropometry and sex~\citep{Henys2021Eigenmetric}. The open biological question is how anatomical variation affects the deformation directions available to the ring, their ability to change canal dimensions, and their recruitment by different loads.

Cross-subject spectral ordering complicates that question. An eigenvalue rank is not an anatomical identity: close eigenvalues permit neighbouring eigenvectors to exchange order or mix, even when their combined span changes less~\citep{Pierre1988,ManconiMace2017}. Comparing only the ninth mode of two subjects could therefore exaggerate a difference in inlet-opening ability if that ability has shifted to the tenth mode in one subject. Invariant-subspace comparisons address the span of such neighbouring modes~\citep{GhoshGhanem2012}. They remove arbitrary within-pair labelling, but do not presume that capacities are identical across people. Spectral gaps, mode assignment, and principal angles establish where a pair is a defensible comparison unit; anatomical diameter functionals establish what it can do.

We study 278 CT-derived pelves with individual geometry and bone elasticity registered to a shared domain~\citep{HenyssKuchar2025BoneDat}. For each of the 15 lowest linearised stiffness modes, we assess spectral correspondence and, for recurrent clusters, the maximum change in inlet, midpelvic, and outlet diameters per millimetre of maximum nodal displacement. This capacity is a kinematic quantity in mm/mm, not force-normalised compliance. We then examine modal strain-energy fractions under two standing and three comparative expansion loads to determine which available directions are recruited. Specifically, we test whether low-rank modes remain ordered, whether label exchange within a higher-rank pair produces a larger apparent rank-level anatomical difference than the pair-level capacity difference, and whether standing and expansion load paths select different parts of the spectrum. The analysis concerns initial elastic response under the specified restraints and material properties.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

We first define the shared-domain FE models and modal comparisons, then the anatomical functionals and load analyses used to interpret them. We distinguish solver rank~$j$, reference label $i=\pi_s^{-1}(j)$ assigned across subjects, and anatomical functional~$\ell$ throughout.


\subsection{Cohort and common-reference geometry}
\label{sec:cohort}

We analysed a precomputed set of $N{=}278$ CT-derived pelvic finite-element models assembled on a common reference domain ($n_F{=}150$, $n_M{=}128$; age 16--91~yr, mean $54\pm19$).
The analysis reuses the published BoneDat cohort.
The source repository provides standardised pelvic geometries suitable for in silico analysis~\citep{HenyssKuchar2025BoneDat}.
The reference pelvic domain $\Omega_{\rm ref}$ was discretized using a conforming volumetric mesh comprising $166\,808$ linear four-node tetrahedral elements (Lagrange $P_1$, $48\,291$ nodes, totaling $n=144\,873$ displacement degrees of freedom), with conforming interfaces between the bony segments (sacrum, left and right ossa coxae) and the joint cartilage domains (bilateral sacroiliac articular cartilage and the pubic symphysis disc).
A nonrigid volumetric registration $\chi_s: \Omega_{\rm ref}\to\Omega_s$ with deformation gradient $F_s(X)=\nabla_X\chi_s(X)$ and Jacobian $J_s(X)=\det F_s(X)$ maps the template domain $\Omega_{\rm ref}$ onto each subject domain $\Omega_s$.
The registration was obtained by radial-basis-function interpolation.
The registered geometries share anatomical correspondence and retain cohort size variation. The primary analysis uses archived solver-order spectra; the allometric analysis examines isotropic size as a covariate.
Fig.~\ref{fig:model-setup} summarises the anatomical dimensions, ligament representation, and boundary-condition layout used to define the pelvic models analysed here.

\subsection{Pullback-assembled linear elasticity on a common reference domain}
\label{sec:pullback}

Let $\Omega_{\rm ref}\subset\mathbb R^3$ be the template domain and $\Omega_s$ the subject domain, related by a fixed volumetric registration $x=\chi_s(X)$ with deformation gradient $F_s(X)=\nabla_X\chi_s(X)$ and local Jacobian $J_s(X)=\det F_s$.
Writing $\chi_s(X)=X+\varphi_s(X)$, so $F_s=I+\nabla_X\varphi_s$, small-strain linear elasticity is solved on $\Omega_s$ but integrated on $\Omega_{\rm ref}$ through the standard change of variables~\citep{MarsdenHughes1983,BonetWood1997}.
For trial and test fields $\widehat{\mathbf u},\widehat{\mathbf v}\in V\subset[H^1(\Omega_{\rm ref})]^3$, the physical strain tensor at $x=\chi_s(X)$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\bm\varepsilon_x(\mathbf u)=\mathrm{sym}(\nabla_x \mathbf u)=\mathrm{sym}\!\big(\nabla_X \widehat{\mathbf u}\,F_s^{-1}\big),
\\
\bm\sigma_x=C_{\rm phys}(x):\bm\varepsilon_x(\mathbf u),
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
and the pullback-assembled elastic bilinear form reads
\begin{equation}
a(\widehat{\mathbf u},\widehat{\mathbf v})
=\int_{\Omega_{\rm ref}}\!\Big[C_{\rm phys}\!\big(\chi_s(X)\big):\mathrm{sym}\!\big(\nabla_X \widehat{\mathbf u}\,F_s^{-1}\big)\Big]
:\mathrm{sym}\!\big(\nabla_X \widehat{\mathbf v}\,F_s^{-1}\big)\,J_s\,\mathrm dX.
\label{eq:pullback-bilinear}
\end{equation}
The fixed image registration supplies $F_s$ and $J_s$ for the small-strain formulation.
The volume-metric bilinear form (denoted by $m$ and assembled as $M_s$) inherits the geometric volume factor,
\begin{equation}
m(\widehat{\mathbf u},\widehat{\mathbf v})
=\int_{\Omega_{\rm ref}} J_s(X)\,\widehat{\mathbf u}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf v}\,\mathrm dX,
\label{eq:mass-subject}
\end{equation}
defining an $L^2$ volume inner product weighted by local volumetric dilation.
The resulting generalised eigenvalues $\lambda_k = \boldsymbol\phi_k^\top K_s \boldsymbol\phi_k / (\boldsymbol\phi_k^\top M_s \boldsymbol\phi_k)$ are stiffness-spectrum descriptors in a volume metric. With coordinates in mm and modulus in MPa, they have units N/mm$^4$. The geometric-volume mass form makes these static stiffness descriptors; their reciprocals have units mm$^4$/N.
The \emph{unmapped} reference mass matrix $M_{\rm ref}^{0}$ is obtained from~\eqref{eq:mass-subject} with $J\equiv 1$ and provides a shared, registration-independent inner product across subjects.


\noindent\textbf{Material model.}
The physical elasticity tensor $C_{\rm phys}(x)$ is isotropic and heterogeneous.
For bone, the CT-derived hydroxyapatite density $\rho(x)$ is converted to ash density $\rho_{\mathrm{ash}}=(\rho+0.09)/1.14$ and mapped to a Young's modulus $E(x)$ using an implemented piecewise approximation motivated by Keller~\citep{Keller1994},
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
E(x)=\begin{cases}
2398\,\mathrm{MPa}, & \rho_{\mathrm{ash}}(x)\le 0.486,\\[2pt]
\alpha\,\rho_{\mathrm{ash}}(x)^{\beta}, & \rho_{\mathrm{ash}}(x)>0.486,
\end{cases}
\\
 \alpha=10\,200,\ \beta=2.0,
\label{eq:keller}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with Poisson's ratio $\nu=0.3$. Negative input densities are clipped to zero. The law is applied at source points, after which the resulting modulus is interpolated to FE cells using RBF interpolation (smoothing parameter 50, ten neighbours). Here $\rho$ and $\rho_{\rm ash}$ denote numerical density values in g/cm$^3$, and $\alpha$ has units of MPa; logarithms of density below refer to the dimensionless numerical value in those units. The constant low-density branch and exponent 2.0 define the implemented approximation to the cited regression. Density--modulus calibration varies by anatomical site~\citep{Morgan2003Trabecular}; the cohort uses one prescribed law.
Cartilage (sacroiliac joints and pubic symphysis) is homogeneous linear elastic with $E=1.0$\,MPa and $\nu=0.45$.
The cartilage parameters are prescribed effective values; pelvic joint mechanics and bone adaptation provide context for this representation~\citep{Frost1987,Huiskes1987,Snijders1993,Vleeming2012,Goode2008}.

\noindent\textbf{Ligaments in pullback coordinates.}
Ligament families are represented as polyline bundles mapped from template nodes $X_i$ to physical points $x_i=\chi_s(X_i)$.
For each segment $(X_i,X_j)$ define the physical unit tangent $\mathbf e=(x_j-x_i)/\|x_j-x_i\|$ and length $L=\|x_j-x_i\|$.
The total bundle stiffness and pretension are distributed across $N_{\rm seg}$ segments, yielding per-segment $k_{\rm seg}=K_{\rm bundle}/N_{\rm seg}$ and $T_{\rm seg}=T_{\rm bundle}/N_{\rm seg}$.
Each segment contributes an axial stiffness block
\begin{equation}
W=k_{\rm seg}\,(\mathbf e\mathbf e^\top),\qquad
K^{\rm ax}_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix} +W & -W\\ -W & +W\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and, in the presence of pretension $T_{\rm seg}\ge 0$, a geometric (prestress) stiffness
\begin{equation}
W_g=\tfrac{T_{\rm seg}}{L}\big(I-\mathbf e\mathbf e^\top\big),\qquad K^{\rm geo}_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}W_g&-W_g\\-W_g&W_g\end{bmatrix}\succeq0,
\label{eq:kgeo}
\end{equation}
together with paired nodal forces $\pm T_{\rm seg}\mathbf e$ on the right-hand side.
The assembled linear stiffness, including prescribed ligament prestress, is
\begin{equation}
K_s=K_{\rm elas,s}+K_{\rm lig,s}+K^{\rm geo}_s(T)+K_{\Gamma,s},\qquad
f=f_{\rm ext}+f_{\rm pret}(T),
\label{eq:K-assembled}
\end{equation}
where geometric stiffness $K^{\rm geo}_s(T)$ incorporates the effect of prescribed ligament pretension $T$ in a linearised configuration.
The term $K_{\Gamma,s}$ implements Dirichlet penalties on the restrained superior sacral facet $\Gamma_{\rm ref}$,
$a_\Gamma(\widehat{\mathbf u},\widehat{\mathbf v})=\gamma\int_{\Gamma_{\rm ref}} \widehat{\mathbf u}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf v}\,J_{\Gamma,s}\,\mathrm dS_X$ with $J_{\Gamma,s}=J_s\|F_s^{-T}N\|$, using a fixed penalty parameter $\gamma = 10^6\,\mathrm{N/mm^3}$.

\noindent\textbf{Force-controlled loading.}
Neumann terms are pulled back to $\Gamma_{\rm ref}$ so that physical resultants are invariant under $\chi_s$:
\begin{equation}
\int_{\Gamma_s}\mathbf t_{\rm phys}\!\cdot\!\mathbf v\,\mathrm dS_x
=\int_{\Gamma_{\rm ref}} J_{\Gamma,s}(X)\,\mathbf t_{\rm phys}(\chi_s(X))\cdot\widehat{\mathbf v}(X)\,\mathrm dS_X.
\label{eq:neumann-pullback}
\end{equation}
All load cases in this study are defined by a total force $\mathbf F$ on a tagged physical region; the applied traction is $\mathbf t_{\rm phys}=\mathbf F/A_{\rm phys}$ with $A_{\rm phys}=\int_{\Gamma_{\rm ref}}J_{\Gamma,s}\,\mathrm dS_X$, so that $\int_{\Gamma_s}\mathbf t_{\rm phys}\,\mathrm dS_x=\mathbf F$ for every subject.

\subsection{Standardised load cases}
\label{sec:loading}

Loads were applied as force-controlled Neumann tractions via~\eqref{eq:neumann-pullback} so that every subject experienced the same integrated force vector regardless of physical surface area.
Two standing cases were considered: symmetric two-leg stance (SP2leg; $400$\,N per acetabulum, $+$CC) and one-leg stance (SP1leg; $800$\,N on the stance acetabulum, $+$CC).
The 400\,N per hip and 800\,N one-leg magnitudes define standardised comparative loads; measured hip-contact forces provide physiological context~\citep{Bergmann2001JBiomech,Kutzner2016PLOS}.
Three comparative pelvic expansion cases probed load-dependent routing: \LABone\ applied bilateral mediolateral internal ring expansion forces ($\pm 400$\,N outward), \LABtwo\ applied bilateral ischial-tuberosity distraction ($\pm 400$\,N), and \LABthree\ applied anteroposterior outlet distraction between the pubis insertion ($-$AP, $400$\,N) and the sacrococcygeal joint ($+$AP, $400$\,N).
The three cases quantify initial pelvic compliance under prescribed force directions.
Static finite-element solutions solve the linear equilibrium equation $K_s \mathbf u = \mathbf b$ with
\begin{equation}
\mathbf b = \mathbf f_{\rm ext} + \mathbf f_{\rm pret}
\label{eq:total-load-vector}
\end{equation}
for the primary total-response analysis, where $\mathbf f_{\rm pret}$ represents baseline ligament pretension ($20$\,N per ligament bundle) and $\mathbf f_{\rm ext}$ represents external load.
Primary cohort-wide modal strain-energy fractions (Section~\ref{sec:load-results}; Table~\ref{tab:dominant-sets}) are computed directly from these total displacement fields $\mathbf u$.
By linearity, total displacement decomposes as $\mathbf u = \mathbf u_{\rm pret} + \delta\mathbf u_{\rm ext}$, where $\delta\mathbf u_{\rm ext} = K_s^{-1}\mathbf f_{\rm ext}$ is the incremental external load response.
Under external load scaling $\mathbf f_{\rm ext} \to c\mathbf f_{\rm ext}$, incremental displacements scale proportionally ($\delta\mathbf u_{\rm ext} \to c\,\delta\mathbf u_{\rm ext}$), such that modal expansion coefficients $q_i = \boldsymbol\phi_i^\top M_s \delta\mathbf u_{\rm ext}$ scale by $c$, modal energies $E_i = \frac{1}{2}\lambda_i q_i^2$ scale by $c^2$, and the normalised modal strain-energy fractions $f_i = E_i / \sum_j E_j$ of the incremental response are strictly scale-invariant.
To examine the effect of baseline pretension on routing, we compared archived total-response and incremental-response modal fractions in 18 subjects with complete stored incremental outputs. The top recruited mode agreed for all five loads in all 18 subjects. The maximum individual change in the 9--10 block fraction was 4.24 percentage points, while its mean change under \LABone\ was $+3.48$ percentage points. The smallest dominant set reaching 80\% of retained energy had the same size in all 18 standing cases and in 14 of 18 \LABone\ cases. Dominant routing was similar in this 18-subject subset.

\subsection{Generalised eigenproblem, rigid-mode filtering, and cross-subject pairing}
\label{sec:evp}

For each subject $s$ we assembled $(K_s,M_s)$ on $\Omega_{\rm ref}$ via~\eqref{eq:K-assembled} and~\eqref{eq:mass-subject} and solved the generalised eigenproblem (the subject index on $\lambda_k$ and $\boldsymbol\phi_k$ is suppressed)
\begin{equation}
K_s\boldsymbol\phi_k=\lambda_k M_s\boldsymbol\phi_k,\qquad
\boldsymbol\phi_i^\top M_s \boldsymbol\phi_j=\delta_{ij},
\label{eq:gep}
\end{equation}
using a Krylov--Schur solver (SLEPc) with shift-invert spectral transform and MUMPS direct factorisation, extracting the $N_{\rm ev}=15$ lowest non-rigid eigenpairs.
Rigid modes (numerically $\lambda<10^{-8}$) were filtered.
For cross-subject comparison we used the mass-weighted modal assurance criterion on the shared reference metric,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAC}_{M_{\rm ref}^0}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=
\frac{|\mathbf x^\top M_{\rm ref}^{0}\mathbf y|^2}
{(\mathbf x^\top M_{\rm ref}^{0}\mathbf x)(\mathbf y^\top M_{\rm ref}^{0}\mathbf y)}\in[0,1],
\label{eq:mac}
\end{equation}
and solved the Hungarian optimal-assignment problem on $-\mathrm{MAC}_{M_{\rm ref}^0}$ over the full $N_{\rm ev}\times N_{\rm ev}$ matrix~\citep{Kuhn1955Hungarian}, yielding an assignment bijection $\pi_s: \{1,\dots,N_{\rm ev}\} \to \{1,\dots,N_{\rm ev}\}$, where $\pi_s(i_{\rm ref}) = j_{\rm rank}$ maps reference identifier $i_{\rm ref}$ to its rank position in subject~$s$, and the inverse permutation $\pi_s^{-1}(j_{\rm rank}) = i_{\rm ref}$ assigns reference identity to each solver rank.
Assigned pairs with $\mathrm{MAC}_{M_{\rm ref}^0}<0.05$ were flagged as unpaired for reporting purposes. This flag indicates a low-confidence match; the assignment remains a bijection.
Rank index~$j$ (solver order) is required for gap statistics and cluster detection; the inverse assignment $\pi_s^{-1}(j)$ maps each solver mode back to its reference identity and is used only for anatomical interpretation, preserving the distinction between rank order and pathway identity emphasised in the Introduction.


\subsection{Spectral gap structure and clustered-regime detection}
\label{sec:gaps}

For each subject with ordered eigenvalues $\lambda_1\le\cdots\le\lambda_{N_{\rm ev}}$, relative consecutive gaps are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{gap}_{\rm in}(i)=\frac{\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i}{\lambda_i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N_{\rm ev}-1.
\label{eq:gap-in}
\end{equation}
A clustered regime $[i..j]$ is defined as the maximal contiguous rank block starting at~$i$ with internal gaps below threshold, $\mathrm{gap}_{\rm in}(k)<\varepsilon_{\rm in}$ for $k=i,\ldots,j-1$.
The threshold is set from data as the 25th percentile of the pooled $\mathrm{gap}_{\rm in}$ distribution across the cohort, yielding $\varepsilon_{\rm in}\approx 0.13$; sensitivity to the percentile choice is reported in Section~\ref{sec:details-D}.
External separation is quantified by
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sep}_{\rm out}([i..j])=\min\!\left(\frac{\lambda_i-\lambda_{i-1}}{\lambda_i},\ \frac{\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_j}{\lambda_j}\right),
\label{eq:sep-out}
\end{equation}
defined for interior clusters ($i > 1$ and $j < N_{\rm ev}$) where both adjacent neighbours exist. For boundary blocks reaching modal truncation (such as the 12--15 cluster with $N_{\rm ev}=15$), the upper neighbour is uncomputed and two-sided separation is formally undefined.
All adjacent relative gaps exceeded the operational multiplicity threshold $\varepsilon_{\rm deg}=10^{-6}$; none met $\mathrm{gap}_{\rm in}\le\varepsilon_{\rm deg}$. The regimes detected by~\eqref{eq:gap-in} are operational small-gap clusters associated with elevated perturbation sensitivity~\citep{Pierre1988,ManconiMace2017}.

\subsection{Invariant subspaces: principal angles, Grassmann distance, and Procrustes alignment}
\label{sec:subspace-methods}

For each clustered rank block of dimension~$r$, the corresponding subject-specific subspace was represented by an $M_{\rm ref}^{0}$-orthonormal basis
\begin{equation}
Q_s\in\mathbb R^{n\times r},\qquad Q_s^\top M_{\rm ref}^{0}\,Q_s=I_r.
\end{equation}
With a fixed reference basis $Q_{\rm ref}$, define the overlap matrix
\begin{equation}
S=Q_{\rm ref}^\top M_{\rm ref}^{0}\,Q_s\in\mathbb R^{r\times r},\qquad S=U\Sigma V^\top,\qquad \cos\theta_k=\sigma_k(S),
\label{eq:overlap}
\end{equation}
with singular values $\sigma_k$ yielding the principal angles $\theta_k\in[0,\pi/2]$ between the two subspaces~\citep{BjorckGolub1973Angles}.
The Grassmann distance is
\begin{equation}
d_G(Q_{\rm ref},Q_s)=\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\theta_k^2\Big)^{1/2}.
\label{eq:grassmann}
\end{equation}
When a stable basis representation inside a clustered subspace is required, we apply an orthogonal Procrustes rotation~\citep{Schoenemann1966Procrustes},
\begin{equation}
\widetilde Q_s=Q_s\,(V U^\top),
\label{eq:procrustes}
\end{equation}
which minimises the $M_{\rm ref}^{0}$-weighted distance $\|Q_{\rm ref} - Q_s R\|_{M_{\rm ref}^0}$ over orthogonal matrices $R\in\mathrm O(r)$ while preserving the subspace $\mathrm{span}(Q_s)$.

We compare subspaces through basis-invariant measures and retain individual-rank couplings as the comparator for label exchange.

\noindent\textbf{Degeneracy vs.\ near-degenerate mixing.}
Exact mathematical multiplicity requires $\lambda_i = \lambda_{i+1}$ (operationalized numerically as $\mathrm{gap}_{\rm in}(k)\le\varepsilon_{\rm deg}$ for all $k$ in the block, with $\varepsilon_{\rm deg}\ll\varepsilon_{\rm in}$); individual eigenvectors inside a degenerate subspace are then not unique, and any unitary rotation inside $\mathrm{span}(Q_s)$ is an equally valid basis.
When $\varepsilon_{\rm deg}<\mathrm{gap}_{\rm in}(k)<\varepsilon_{\rm in}$ the eigenvalues are close but distinct; each eigenvector is unique up to sign, yet small perturbations can rotate the internal basis substantially while the spanned subspace can be less sensitive if its external separation is sufficiently large relative to the perturbation. Anatomical coupling is therefore evaluated directly with the functionals below.

\subsection{Canonical anatomical functionals inside clustered subspaces}
\label{sec:canonical-methods}

To interpret clustered subspaces biologically, we use basis-invariant linear functionals defined by anatomical landmark pairs.
On the reference template mesh, five key obstetric and locomotor diameters are defined:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{AP inlet} ($s_{\rm AP}$): Promontory ($X_{\rm prom}$) to superior pubic symphysis ($X_{\rm pub}$), with unit direction $\mathbf e_{\rm AP} = (X_{\rm pub} - X_{\rm prom}) / \|X_{\rm pub} - X_{\rm prom}\|$;
\item \textbf{ML inlet} ($s_{\rm ML}$): Widest transverse diameter across left and right iliopectineal lines ($X_{\rm left}, X_{\rm right}$), with unit direction $\mathbf e_{\rm ML} = (X_{\rm right} - X_{\rm left}) / \|X_{\rm right} - X_{\rm left}\|$;
\item \textbf{Interspinous midpelvic diameter (BIS)} ($s_{\rm BIS}$): Left to right ischial spine ($X_{\rm sp,left}, X_{\rm sp,right}$), with unit direction $\mathbf e_{\rm BIS} = (X_{\rm sp,right} - X_{\rm sp,left}) / \|X_{\rm sp,right} - X_{\rm sp,left}\|$;
\item \textbf{Bituberous outlet} ($s_{\rm BIT}$): Left to right inferior ischial tuberosity ($X_{\rm tub,left}, X_{\rm tub,right}$), with unit direction $\mathbf e_{\rm BIT} = (X_{\rm tub,right} - X_{\rm tub,left}) / \|X_{\rm tub,right} - X_{\rm tub,left}\|$;
\item \textbf{AP outlet} ($s_{\rm OUTLETAP}$): Sacrococcygeal joint ($X_{\rm cocc}$) to inferior pubic symphysis ($X_{\rm pub,inf}$), with unit direction $\mathbf e_{\rm OUTAP} = (X_{\rm pub,inf} - X_{\rm cocc}) / \|X_{\rm pub,inf} - X_{\rm cocc}\|$.
\end{enumerate}
For each subject $s$, $+$ denotes the target landmark and $-$ the origin. Their physical positions are $x_{\ell,\pm}^{(s)}=\chi_s(X_{\ell,\pm})$, and $\mathbf e_\ell^{(s)}=(x_{\ell,+}^{(s)}-x_{\ell,-}^{(s)})/\|x_{\ell,+}^{(s)}-x_{\ell,-}^{(s)}\|$. The modal displacement degrees of freedom $\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^n$ remain indexed on the reference mesh. Let $\mathcal I_{\ell,\pm}\mathbf u$ denote inverse-distance-squared interpolation from the 32 nearest reference nodes to the corresponding template landmark $X_{\ell,\pm}$. The implemented functional is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
s_\ell^{(s)}(\mathbf u)=\mathbf e_\ell^{(s)\top}\big(\mathcal I_{\ell,+}\mathbf u-\mathcal I_{\ell,-}\mathbf u\big)=\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)\top}\mathbf u,\qquad\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)}\in\mathbb R^n,\\
\ell\in\{\text{AP, ML, BIS, BIT, OUTLETAP}\}.
\label{eq:functional-score}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The interpolation weights are fixed across subjects; the direction $\mathbf e_\ell^{(s)}$ is updated from RBF-mapped landmark positions. BIS measures the midpelvis between the ischial spines, whereas BIT measures the intertuberous outlet. The AP-outlet observable runs from the sacrococcygeal landmark to the inferior pubic symphysis.

\noindent\textbf{Basis-invariant max-nodal anatomical capacity.}
For any individual functional $\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)}$ and an orthonormal basis $Q_s \in \mathbb R^{n \times r}$ spanning a clustered subspace of dimension $r$ ($Q_s^\top M_{\rm ref}^0 Q_s = I_r$), every displacement in the subspace is parameterised by $\mathbf u = Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha$ with $\boldsymbol\alpha \in \mathbb R^r$.
The maximum anatomical diameter response per 1\,mm maximum nodal displacement is defined by the constrained max-nodal capacity:
\begin{equation}
\kappa_{\ell,\infty}(Q_s) = \max_{\boldsymbol\alpha \ne \mathbf 0} \frac{|\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)\top} Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha|}{\max_a \|(Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha)_a\|_2} = \max_{\boldsymbol\alpha} |\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)\top} Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha| \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \max_a \|(Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha)_a\|_2 \le 1,
\label{eq:single-functional-max-nodal}
\end{equation}
where $a$ indexes the finite-element mesh nodes and $\|(Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha)_a\|_2$ denotes the Euclidean displacement norm of node $a$.
Because both the numerator and the max-nodal constraint depend only on the represented displacement field $\mathbf u = Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha$, this quantity is strictly invariant under arbitrary internal orthogonal basis rotations $\widetilde Q_s = Q_s R$ with $R \in \mathrm O(r)$, since $\widetilde Q_s (R^\top \boldsymbol\alpha) = Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha$.
For $r=2$, homogeneity allows parameterisation of $\boldsymbol\alpha$ by polar angle $\theta\in[0,\pi)$ with $\boldsymbol\alpha(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^\top$; the maximum is approximated from 90 angular samples followed by scalar refinement. For $r\ge3$, we solve the equivalent convex program $\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha} \mathbf c^\top\boldsymbol\alpha$ subject to $\|(Q_s\boldsymbol\alpha)_a\|_2\le1$ at every node, where $\mathbf c=Q_s^\top\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)}$; the sign symmetry removes the absolute value. Constraint generation starts with 16 nodes, solves each reduced problem with SLSQP and analytic Jacobians, adds the most violated mesh node, and terminates only when the full-mesh maximum squared norm is at most $1+10^{-8}$. The objective is linear and every constraint is convex, so a feasible stationary solution is a global optimum of the finite-dimensional problem up to numerical tolerance. Four independent full-constraint optimisations provide an additional check.
The optimal displacement field $\boldsymbol\psi_\ell^{(s)} = Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha^* / \max_a \|(Q_s \boldsymbol\alpha^*)_a\|_2$ is normalised so that $\max_a \|\boldsymbol\psi_{\ell,a}^{(s)}\|_2 = 1$ numerically, and the resulting anatomical capacity $\kappa_{\ell,\infty}(Q_s) = |\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)\top}\boldsymbol\psi_\ell^{(s)}|$ has physical units of millimetres diameter change per millimetre maximum nodal displacement.

\noindent\textbf{Multivariate canonical decomposition.}
When joint coupling across a pair of measurement vectors $(\mathbf b_1^{(s)}, \mathbf b_2^{(s)})$ (e.g., AP and ML inlet, or BIS and BIT outlet) is examined, we assemble the covector matrix $B_s = [\mathbf b_1^{(s)}\ \ \mathbf b_2^{(s)}] \in \mathbb R^{n \times 2}$ and its subspace projection:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
C_s = Q_s^\top B_s = \big[Q_s^\top\mathbf b_1^{(s)}\ \ Q_s^\top\mathbf b_2^{(s)}\big] \in \mathbb R^{r \times 2}, \\
 C_s = U_s \Sigma_s V_s^\top,
\label{eq:canonical-proj}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the subject-specific singular values $\sigma_{s,1}\ge\sigma_{s,2}$ describe joint coupling under the coefficient constraint $\|\boldsymbol\alpha\|_2=1$, equivalent to unit norm in $M_{\rm ref}^0$. Max-nodal capacities are evaluated separately by Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-functional-max-nodal}.
Let $\mathbf u_{s,k}$ and $\mathbf v_{s,k}$ be column $k$ of $U_s$ and $V_s$, respectively. The joint-SVD displacement directions are $\boldsymbol\zeta_k^{(s)}=Q_s\mathbf u_{s,k}$ ($k=1,2$), with corresponding dual functionals $\widetilde{\mathbf b}_k^{(s)}=B_s\mathbf v_{s,k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}(V_s)_{\ell k}\mathbf b_\ell^{(s)}$. Their action is $\widetilde{\mathbf b}_k^{(s)\top}\boldsymbol\zeta_k^{(s)}=\sigma_{s,k}$.
The singular-direction scores subsequently normalised by maximum nodal displacement are $\widehat s_k^{(s)}=\sigma_{s,k}/\max_a\|\boldsymbol\zeta_{k,a}^{(s)}\|_2$.
Because $V_s\in\mathrm O(2)$ mixes the two anatomical axes, the first joint direction weights the first observable more strongly only when $|(V_s)_{11}|\ge |(V_s)_{21}|$. Across the pelvic cohort, the first joint direction in the 9--10 subspace weights the transverse (ML) observable more strongly in $99.6\%$ of subjects.
For axis-specific evaluation of anatomical opening, the basis-invariant single-functional capacity $\kappa_{\ell,\infty}(Q_s)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-functional-max-nodal}) serves as our primary quantitative metric, while joint-SVD scores provide complementary bivariate structure.

The reference-mode displacement atlas and its polynomial decomposition are given in Appendix~\ref{app:atlas}.

\subsection{Load routing and modal strain-energy fractions}
\label{sec:routing-methods}

For each load case we solved the static problem $K_s\mathbf u_s=\mathbf b$ on $\Omega_{\rm ref}$. With $\Phi_s=[\boldsymbol\phi_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol\phi_{N_{\rm ev}}]$ denoting the $M_s$-orthonormal retained modes, we projected the response as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q_s=\Phi_s^\top M_s\mathbf u_s,\qquad E_{s,i}=\tfrac12\lambda_i q_{s,i}^2,\qquad f_{s,i}=\frac{E_{s,i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm ev}}E_{s,j}}.
\label{eq:modal-fraction}
\end{equation}
The load-case index and the suppressed subject index on $\lambda_i$ are implicit. We write $f_i$ below when the subject and load are clear. The $E_{s,i}$ partition the quadratic energy of the retained projection under the full linearised $K_s$, including its ligament-geometric and boundary-penalty contributions; $f_{s,i}$ is normalised over the $N_{\rm ev}=15$ computed modes.
Block throughput for a clustered regime $C$ is $f_C=\sum_{i\in C}f_i$, and the dominant mode set $N_{80}$ of a load case is the smallest subset of modes (selected in descending order of mean energy contribution) whose cumulative mean energy fractions reach at least 0.80.


First-order material-parameter sensitivity of eigenvalues and adjacent gaps is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:uq}.

\subsection{Decomposition of variability sources and robustness analyses}
\label{sec:robustness-methods}

To separate shape and material contributions to spectral variability, we compared the full cohort against a shape-only cohort ($\chi_s$, fixed reference bone modulus) and a material-only cohort (identity deformation, subject-specific bone modulus).
Robustness was assessed by random permutation of subject order and 2,000 full-cohort bootstrap draws with replacement, re-estimating the cluster threshold in each draw.
Exploratory allometric regressions and the variance decomposition of eigenvalues are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:allometry}.

\subsection{Analytical interpretation of a close eigenvalue pair}
For a symmetric two-coordinate stiffness matrix $K_2=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}k_1&c\\c&k_2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$, the eigenvalue separation is $2\sqrt{((k_1-k_2)/2)^2+c^2}$. Near $k_1=k_2$, a small change in detuning can rotate the two eigenvectors while their joint span remains unchanged. Appendix~\ref{app:toy} gives the full three-coordinate illustration and its stochastic implementation.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:statistics}

All statistical analyses were executed using SciPy, statsmodels, and custom Python routines.
Continuous metrics across non-normal cohorts were evaluated using non-parametric methods.
Two-group comparisons (e.g., non-swapped vs.\ swapped cluster members, or male vs.\ female distributions) were conducted using two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ tests.
Effect sizes for Mann--Whitney tests are reported as rank-biserial correlations $r_{\rm rb}=1-2U_1/(n_1n_2)$, where $U_1$ is computed for the first listed group. With this orientation, positive values indicate larger observations in the second group; the statistic equals Cliff\textquotesingle s delta for second versus first group.
Bivariate associations between continuous anatomical dimensions (such as AP inlet diameter or isotropic scale) and modal metrics (energy fractions, spectral gaps) were quantified using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient $\rho_s$.
The four rank-versus-capacity comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:detail-B3} were adjusted with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure. Exploratory sex-group tests are reported without multiplicity adjustment.
Confidence intervals for median differences in the label-exchange comparisons were obtained by percentile bootstrap resampling (5,000 independent within-group resamples; random seed 20260918), using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Mann--Whitney probabilities compare distributions; bootstrap intervals quantify median differences.
Cluster-prevalence intervals used 2,000 bootstrap replicates of 278 draws with replacement and threshold re-estimation (seed 20260918). These intervals describe resampling variability within the cohort.

Differences in subspace orientation across the cohort were quantified using the Grassmann distance $d_G(Q_{\rm ref}, Q_s) = \|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2$.
The Grassmann distance $d_G$ measures between-subspace orientation independently of internal basis indexing. Sex-group distances use the selected reference.

\subsection{Software and computational implementation}


Finite-element assembly, eigensolves, and postprocessing were implemented in Python~3.11 with DOLFINx/FEniCSx~($\ge$0.10), PETSc, and SLEPc. Quadrature points and weights were obtained with Basix~0.10.0, the finite-element library used by FEniCSx.
Study analysis scripts produced the figures, tables, and numerical results. Intermediate outputs are stored as compressed Zarr arrays.

\subsection{Population variability separates an order-locked backbone from recurrent clustered regimes}
\label{sec:spectral-results}

The cohort of $N{=}278$ subjects resolves the spectrum into two regimes: an order-locked low-rank backbone and recurrent higher-rank clustered blocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectral-dashboard}, Table~\ref{tab:clusters}).
The complete reference deformation atlas is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:atlas}; Fig.~\ref{fig:reference-contrast} shows its low- and higher-rank anatomical contrast in the main text. Modes~1--3 are rarely reordered across the cohort (swap rate $<0.4\%$), whereas higher ranks contain small consecutive gaps and frequent label exchange.
Using a data-driven cluster threshold $\varepsilon_{\rm in}\approx 0.131$ (25th percentile of pooled relative consecutive gaps; see Methods), we detect nine near-degenerate rank clusters above a 3\% prevalence floor.
The most prevalent cluster is rank modes~9--10 (\PrevalenceNineTen\% of subjects), followed by rank modes~12--15 (\PrevalenceTwelveFifteen\%) and rank modes~5--6 (\PrevalenceFiveSix\%).
All observed adjacent gaps exceeded the operational multiplicity threshold $\varepsilon_{\rm deg}=10^{-6}$.

The low-rank and higher-rank spectral regions show different exchange frequencies.
Label fragility is concentrated in a small number of compact families, while low-rank order is preserved.
The 9--10 and 12--15 blocks have small median internal gaps and show label exchange across the archived cohort. These are cross-sectional exchange observations.
The 9--10 block has a larger median external separation than internal gap, whereas the 12--15 regime is left-separated but right-truncated by the eigensolve; the 9--10 pair can therefore be assessed as an interior subspace, whereas conclusions about isolation of 12--15 remain limited.
The result identifies spectral regions in which rank-based comparison is more or less reliable under the present model assumptions.


In the single reference pelvis, modes~1 and~2 show a large component of whole-ring displacement in the anterior and cranial views, whereas modes~9 and~10 show more conspicuous internal change in ring shape (Fig.~\ref{fig:reference-contrast}). After projection of infinitesimal whole-pelvis rigid motion, the nonrigid components account for 3.3\% and 5.5\% of total squared displacement norm in modes~1 and~2, compared with 92.1\% and 95.7\% in modes~9 and~10 (Table~\ref{tab:detail-E1}). These are reference-geometry kinematic proportions, not deformation-energy fractions or measurements of joint strain.

\subsection{Subspace capacity varies less than individual-rank coupling}
\label{sec:label-results}

The empirical question is whether anatomical capacity differs less between exchange groups when evaluated over a subspace than at an individual rank.
This hypothesis was tested for the most prevalent cluster, rank modes~9--10 (Fig.~\ref{fig:label-stability}).

Among the \ClusterMemberN\ cluster members, \ClusterNoSwapN\ showed no exchange and \ClusterSwapN\ showed exchange. Median AP-inlet coupling at rank 9 decreased from \RankNineNo\ to \RankNineYes\,mm/mm (difference \RankNineDifference; 95\% confidence interval [\RankNineLow, \RankNineHigh]; $p=\RankNinePformatted$), whereas rank-10 coupling increased from \RankTenNo\ to \RankTenYes\,mm/mm ($p=\RankTenPformatted$). Thus, the anatomical response redistributed between neighbouring ranks.

The corresponding subspace capacity changed from \ScalarAPNo\ to \ScalarAPYes\,mm/mm (difference \ScalarAPDifference; 95\% confidence interval [\ScalarAPLow, \ScalarAPHigh]; rank-biserial correlation \ScalarAPRankBiserial). This is approximately a 5\% median decrease, compared with approximately 69\% at rank 9. The Mann--Whitney test for the capacity distributions gives $p=\ScalarAPPformatted$ (Benjamini--Hochberg-adjusted $q=0.040$); the bootstrap interval estimates the median difference.

Across all \CohortN\ subjects, irrespective of cluster membership, subspace capacity differed by \CohortScalarAPDifference\,mm/mm (95\% confidence interval [\CohortScalarAPLow, \CohortScalarAPHigh]; $p=\CohortScalarAPPformatted$), while rank-level coupling again redistributed strongly. The between-group capacity contrast is smaller than the rank-level contrast. Cluster-member estimates, effect sizes, and adjusted probabilities are provided in Table~\ref{tab:detail-B3}.

The anatomical comparison concerns capacity, whereas load routing measures actual recruitment under prescribed forces (Fig.~\ref{fig:si-routing}). Subspace orientation and the internal 9--10 gap vary across subjects, and the block's energy fraction under \LAB\ is associated with morphology in a load-specific way.
For \LABone, block throughput decreases with larger AP inlet diameter ($\rho_s{=}{-}0.48$, $p{=}4.3\times10^{-17}$), whereas \LABtwo\ and \LABthree\ show weaker and differently signed trends.

\subsection{Standing and proxy loads distinguish backbone-dominated routing from higher-rank recruitment}
\label{sec:load-results}

The prescribed loads recruit different parts of the retained modal spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:fractions-panel}, Table~\ref{tab:dominant-sets}). Standing is dominated by modes~1--3.
Symmetric two-leg standing (SP2leg) is concentrated in mode~2 alone, which carries about 84.5\% of the retained modal strain energy; single-leg standing (SP1leg) distributes energy across the full rotational triad (modes~1--3), reaching a top-3 cumulative share of about 92.0\%.
Under these two prescribed standing loads, a few low-rank modes account for most of the retained modal energy.

The proxy load family recruits higher-rank modes.
\LABone, bilateral mediolateral internal ring expansion ($\pm 400$\,N outward), preferentially engages the rank~9--10 subspace, which carries a mean energy share of about 0.51 and includes the reference mode with strong AP-inlet coupling.
\LABtwo, bilateral ischial-tuberosity distraction, reroutes energy into the mid-rank block at modes~4--8, where the 5--6 outlet-related pair becomes especially important.
\LABthree, anteroposterior outlet distraction, is hybrid, retaining sizeable low-rank contributions while also recruiting mid-rank and 9--10 pathways.
Thus, loading geometry changes the distribution of retained modal energy.

Among the recurrent clustered regimes, 9--10 remains the clearest case because it combines high cohort prevalence, AP-inlet coupling alongside other canal capacities, direct label-versus-subspace evidence (Fig.~\ref{fig:label-stability}), and substantial recruitment under \LABone.
The 5--6 pair provides a secondary contrast under \LABtwo. The 12--15 block is common across subjects, but none of the selected loads establishes it as one dominant pathway.
Primary cohort routing is evaluated on the total static response. Archived incremental outputs for 18 subjects showed unchanged top-mode identity across all five loads, but individual 9--10 block fractions shifted by up to 4.24 percentage points and dominant-set size changed in four \LABone\ cases (Section~\ref{sec:loading}).
Exploratory sex-group and capacity--load associations are reported in Section~\ref{sec:details-B} and Appendix~\ref{app:capacity-load}.

The three prescribed traction patterns compare recruitment across pelvic loading geometries in the linear model.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Available deformation and load-selected motion}
Standing and ring-expansion tractions recruit different directions in the same constrained pelvic structure. In symmetric two-leg stance, mode~2 accounts for approximately 84.5\% of strain energy among the 15 retained modes; in one-leg stance, modes~1--3 together account for approximately 92.0\%. Bilateral internal-ring expansion instead places approximately 51\% in the 9--10 pair (Fig.~\ref{fig:fractions-panel}). The reference atlas (Fig.~\ref{fig:reference-contrast}) gives this spectral contrast a physical interpretation: only 3.3\% and 5.5\% of the squared displacement norm of reference modes~1 and~2 remains after projecting out whole-pelvis infinitesimal rigid motion, compared with 92.1\% and 95.7\% for modes~9 and~10 (Appendix~\ref{app:atlas}, Table~\ref{tab:detail-E1}). In the reference geometry, the standing-dominant low modes thus resemble large-scale ring motion with relatively little internal shape change, whereas the expansion-recruited pair contains much more internal deformation. This atlas describes one reference, not a population energy decomposition. Its rigid-motion projection does not make these positive-stiffness, constrained modes free rigid-body modes. Together with cohort energy fractions, it suggests why the same anatomy can transmit stance loads and possess directions capable of changing canal dimensions. The distinction accords with the pelvis's roles in load transfer and enclosing a mobile canal~\citep{Vleeming2012,Reitter2014}.

\subsection{Anatomical response despite changes in spectral identity}
The 9--10 pair shows why functional interpretation cannot rest on eigenvalue rank alone. Among its 152 cluster members, 95 had no label exchange and 57 had exchange. Median AP-inlet coupling assigned to rank~9 was 0.454 and 0.139\,mm/mm in these groups, respectively, an approximately 69\% decrease. Yet maximum AP response available within the pair was 0.551 and 0.525\,mm/mm, an approximately 5\% decrease (Fig.~\ref{fig:label-stability}, Table~\ref{tab:detail-B3}). Much of the rank-specific contrast therefore reflects redistribution between neighbouring directions, while the pair retains a more similar kinematic ability to change that diameter. The pair-level difference is nevertheless detectable ($q=0.040$); neither identical capacity nor statistical equivalence follows. The comparison is between exchange-defined groups, not a causal effect of label exchange on anatomy. Modes~1--3 exchange order in fewer than 0.4\% of subjects, supporting individual low-rank descriptors, whereas the 12--15 block ends at the eigensolver boundary and cannot be claimed isolated above rank~15. Subspace capacity is independent of the basis chosen within each person's pair, but this mathematical invariance does not guarantee cross-person equality. For morphologically diverse canals~\citep{BettiManica2018}, a different modal label need not imply loss of an anatomical response pathway, yet a measured capacity difference still requires interpretation on its own scale.

The 9--10 pair has substantial measured capacities for mediolateral inlet, interspinous, bituberous, and AP-outlet diameters as well as AP inlet (Fig.~\ref{fig:subspace-dashboard}); it is neither a pure inlet mode nor a birth-specific module. The regional geometry of the canal matters~\citep{StansfieldCanal2021}. Each reported maximum is obtained by separately optimising one diameter functional within the pair. These maxima do not demonstrate simultaneous enlargement of every diameter, and the signed displacement required for one maximum may oppose another. Capacity in mm/mm describes possible diameter change under a displacement normalisation, not compliance in mm/N or a predicted clinical opening.

\subsection{Why capacity does not predict recruitment by itself}
The 5--6 pair illustrates the distinction between what a subspace permits and what a force excites. It is important under ischial-tuberosity distraction (\LABtwo), although 9--10 has higher interspinous and bituberous capacities (Figs.~\ref{fig:subspace-dashboard} and~\ref{fig:fractions-panel}). In the linear model, each modal response depends on its stiffness and on the projection of the applied force onto that mode. The diameter response then combines signed modal contributions, which can reinforce or cancel. High maximum capacity consequently cannot identify the pair carrying the greatest strain energy or producing the greatest actual diameter change under a specified load. The \LABone\ recruitment of 9--10, \LABtwo\ recruitment of 5--6, and mixed routing under \LABthree\ make this force dependence concrete. Energy shares partition the 15 retained modes; they are not shares of canal opening.

Exploratory cluster-member associations point in the same direction without establishing a universal mapping. Under \LABone, recruitment of 9--10 correlates with its ML-inlet capacity ($\rho_s=0.69$), but weakly with its AP-inlet capacity ($\rho_s=0.12$); under \LABtwo, recruitment of 5--6 correlates with its interspinous capacity ($\rho_s=0.76$; Appendix~\ref{app:capacity-load}). Which capacity tracks energy therefore depends on direction and loading site. These correlations use block energy fractions, not absolute diameter changes, and cannot resolve signed modal combinations in the static response. In the full cohort, AP inlet diameter correlates negatively with relative 9--10 recruitment under \LABone\ ($\rho_s=-0.48$; Fig.~\ref{fig:si-routing}). Initial morphology is thus associated with load routing under this particular traction. This does not show that a narrower inlet opens more in absolute terms: relative energy, baseline size, and displacement are different quantities, and the association is neither adjusted nor causal.

\subsection{Pelvic diversity and functional context}
Canal shape varies across populations, body sizes, and ages~\citep{BettiManica2018,FischerMitteroecker2015,Huseynov2016}. Our analysis adds available and recruited deformation to unloaded anatomy. The smaller AP-capacity contrast of the 9--10 pair than of rank~9 alone shows that a changed spectral label can exaggerate an anatomical difference. Distinct recruitment under the prescribed loads shows why available capacity does not itself specify loaded function. A hypothesis for cross-anatomy testing is that differently shaped pelves may share some available canal-deformation directions but differ in their stiffness and recruitment by the same force. Comparing capacities, stiffness, and responses under matched loads would test this; the present pair-level result does not establish functional compensation.

The repertoire is regional and directional. Internal-ring expansion recruits 9--10; ischial distraction recruits 5--6 despite higher 9--10 interspinous and bituberous capacities. Capacity--recruitment associations also vary by diameter and load (Appendix~\ref{app:capacity-load}). Specific canal dimensions are more informative than pelvic width. Regional canal geometry bears distinct mechanical demands~\citep{StansfieldCanal2021}, while positional MRI records midpelvic and outlet changes~\citep{Reitter2014}. Together they motivate testing whether posture and force direction select different available deformations. Signed changes in several diameters under defined postures and loads could be compared with predicted displacement fields; separately optimised capacities do not imply simultaneous opening.

Regional bony-ring motion may change positions of muscle and fascia attachments and hence pelvic-floor boundary conditions. The deformation fields provide a basis for estimating attachment motions in a future coupled model of the bony ring and pelvic-floor tissues. Pelvic-floor simulations indicate that canal geometry affects tissue deformation under support loads~\citep{StansfieldFloor2021,StansfieldCanal2021}. A coupled model, checked against measured attachment motion and tissue strain under defined loading, could test whether distinct regional ring motions yield different soft-tissue responses. These results motivate testable hypotheses about load-selected ring motion and pelvic-floor tissue deformation.

\subsection{Variation sources, scope, and validation}
Exploratory sex-group contrasts in distance from a reference subspace concern orientation in a common metric (Section~\ref{sec:details-B}); they do not show greater canal compliance or biological advantage in either group. Size, sex, and age can covary with shape in this mixed cohort of 150 women and 128 men aged 16--91 years (mean 54~$\pm$~19). The shape-only and material-only eigenvalue coefficients of variation describe two restricted datasets, whereas the material-uncertainty appendix propagates prescribed parameter covariance through first-order eigenvalue sensitivities. Neither is a decomposition of developmental or evolutionary causes. The analytical close-eigenvalue example (Appendix~\ref{app:toy}) demonstrates possible label redistribution under its chosen parameters; it supplies no additional population evidence.

The anatomical inferences depend on the FE idealisation. The analyses do not determine pelvic-floor performance, obstetric outcome, or evolutionary causes. The superior sacrum is restrained; cartilage, ligaments, and bone follow prescribed properties; the response is linearised and lacks individual ligament laxity, nonlinear joint contact, active muscle forces, and pelvic-floor support. These choices can change mode shapes, capacities, and force projections~\citep{Vleeming2012,Henys2022Ligaments}. Sensitivity to the superior-sacral penalty remains unquantified. Fifteen-mode displacement reconstruction errors are small for tested cases, but small displacement error does not ensure complete energy capture: expanded spectra in three subjects captured 99.3--99.5\% of quadratic energy for standing and 76.5--94.9\% for expansion loads. The reported energy percentages always have the 15-mode denominator. Targeted validation would measure three-dimensional ring and canal-landmark displacements under controlled standing-like and ring-expansion loads in specimens with characterised joints and ligaments, then compare predicted regional directions and relative recruitment.

\section{Conclusions}
Across 278 pelvic FE models, standing preferentially recruited low-rank directions associated with largely whole-ring movement in the reference atlas, whereas internal-ring expansion recruited the higher-rank 9--10 pair with greater internal shape change. Within that recurrent cluster, AP-inlet coupling changed much more at a single rank than the available capacity of the pair, although the pair-level difference remained detectable. Ischial distraction recruited 5--6 despite higher canal-diameter capacities in 9--10. The anatomically variable pelvis thus has available directions of canal change whose expression depends on stiffness and loading; comparing their complete subspaces prevents a change in spectral rank from being mistaken for a comparably large change in anatomical capacity.

\section{Reference-mode polynomial deformation atlas}\label{app:atlas}
\subsection{Methods: whole-pelvis deformation decomposition}\label{sec:mechanisms-methods}
To distinguish global ring kinematics from joint-localised strains, we decompose the reference displacement fields after removing infinitesimal rigid motion. The fractions below partition the nonrigid squared displacement norm within a specified polynomial dictionary. Synthetic checks, the reference-mode atlas, and complete profile tables follow below.

\subsubsection{Joint strain and global ring kinematics}
The pelvic ring is a closed, doubly-curved structural biomechanical loop composed of three major bony segments (the sacrum and bilateral coxal bones) joined anteriorly at the pubic symphysis and posteriorly at the bilateral sacroiliac joints (SIJs).
In continuum mechanics, the standard approach to characterise modal kinematics would be to evaluate the infinitesimal Cauchy strain tensor field,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\mathbf u) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\nabla \mathbf u + \nabla \mathbf u^\top\big).
\label{eq:cauchy-strain}
\end{equation}
In the present quasi-static finite-element formulation, an effective joint modulus of $E_{\rm cartilage} = \CartilageEffectiveModulus\,\mathrm{MPa}$ is prescribed for the sacroiliac cartilage cleft and pubic symphysis disc.
The prescribed cartilage modulus is much lower than the representative bone moduli ($E_{\rm bone} \sim 5\text{--}18\,\mathrm{GPa}$). In these models, strain concentrates near the compliant joints: projection of modal Cauchy strain onto low-order polynomial strain fields leaves 97--99\% of the strain norm unexplained across the 15 reference modes. Such a strain-based description therefore captures little of the global ring motion of interest here.

For Mode~2, a tested one-axis dictionary left 84\% of the nonrigid squared displacement norm unexplained, motivating a three-dimensional basis that represents coupled flexure and shear.
We therefore analyse displacement over the full three-dimensional pelvic domain. After removing infinitesimal rigid motion, we project the remaining field onto four polynomial deformation families. Their unexplained residual remains explicit.

\subsubsection{Continuum inner product and tetrahedral quadrature}
Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the reference pelvic continuum domain with volume $V = \int_\Omega \mathrm{d}V$.
The natural inner product on the displacement space $[L^2(\Omega)]^3$ weighted by volume is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\langle \mathbf u, \mathbf v \rangle_V = \frac{1}{V} \int_\Omega \mathbf u(\mathbf x) \cdot \mathbf v(\mathbf x) \, \mathrm{d}V, \\
\|\mathbf u\|_V^2 = \langle \mathbf u, \mathbf u \rangle_V.
\label{eq:volume-inner-product}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The continuous reference domain $\Omega$ is discretized into an unstructured tetrahedral mesh $\mathcal{T}_h = \{K_e\}_{e=1}^{N_e}$ using piecewise-linear ($P_1$) Lagrange continuous finite elements.
Within any tetrahedron $K$, a $P_1$ displacement field $\mathbf u_h(\mathbf x)$ is an affine vector function of position ($\mathbf u_h|_K \in \mathcal{P}_1(K)$).
When computing projections against quadratic deformation modes $\mathbf p_2 \in \mathcal{P}_2(K)$, the modal cross-product integrand $\mathbf u_h \cdot \mathbf p_2$ is a cubic polynomial ($\mathcal{P}_3(K)$), and the dictionary Gram matrix integrand $\mathbf p_2 \cdot \mathbf p_2$ is a quartic polynomial ($\mathcal{P}_4(K)$).

The four-point tetrahedral rule integrates squared $P_1$ displacement exactly ($\mathcal P_2(K)$). Products of quadratic dictionary fields require degree-4 exactness.
For the reference atlas and its accompanying table, we used a positive 14-point tetrahedral quadrature rule exact through polynomial degree 4.
For a rule exact to polynomial degree $d$ ($d=2$ for four points, $d=4$ for 14 points), the quadrature points $\mathbf x_{K,q}$ and strictly positive weights $w_{K,q} > 0$ satisfy $\sum_{q=1}^{N_{q,\rm tet}} w_{K,q} = |K|$:
\begin{equation}
\int_K p(\mathbf x) \, \mathrm{d}V = \sum_{q=1}^{N_{q,\rm tet}} w_{K,q} \, p(\mathbf x_{K,q}) \qquad \forall p \in \mathcal{P}_d(K).
\end{equation}
Summing across all $N_e$ tetrahedra gives $N_q = N_{q,\rm tet} N_e$ discrete sampling points with weights $w_p > 0$ ($N_q = 4 N_e$ for the 4-point rule; $N_q = 14 N_e$ for the degree-4 rule).
Defining normalised quadrature weights $\tilde w_p = w_p / \sum_{j=1}^{N_q} w_j$, the continuum inner product~\eqref{eq:volume-inner-product} is evaluated as:
\begin{equation}
\langle \mathbf u, \mathbf v \rangle_V \approx \sum_{p=1}^{N_q} \tilde w_p \, \mathbf u(\mathbf x_p) \cdot \mathbf v(\mathbf x_p).
\label{eq:quadrature-sum}
\end{equation}
The degree-4 rule integrates the products of quadratic dictionary functions exactly on each tetrahedron. Because the reference modes are $P_1$, it also integrates their cross-products with the dictionary exactly.
The degree-4 rule integrates the quadratic-dictionary projection exactly on each tetrahedron and supplies positive weights for the inner product. A four-point, degree-2 sensitivity recomputation of all 15 reference modes changed the deformation-family and residual fractions by at most $1.91\times 10^{-5}$ percentage points.
Figure~\ref{fig:quadrature-method} links the element rule to the whole-domain inner product and to the degrees of the three products used in the decomposition.

\subsubsection{Infinitesimal rigid-body motion elimination}
Infinitesimal rigid-body displacements in $\mathbb{R}^3$ constitute a 6-dimensional linear Lie algebra subspace $\mathfrak{se}(3)$ consisting of 3 translations and 3 rotations:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf u_{\rm rigid}(\mathbf x) = \mathbf t + \boldsymbol\omega \times (\mathbf x - \mathbf x_c), \\
 \mathbf t, \boldsymbol\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3,
\label{eq:rigid-field}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf x_c$ is the volume-weighted spatial centroid of the pelvic domain,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x_c = \frac{1}{V}\int_\Omega \mathbf x\,\mathrm dV = \sum_{p=1}^{N_q} \tilde w_p \, \mathbf x_p.
\end{equation}
To ensure scale-free, dimensionally consistent kinematics across subjects, we define the reference gyration radius $r_g$ and non-dimensionalised anatomical coordinates $\tilde{\mathbf x} = (\tilde x, \tilde y, \tilde z)^\top$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
r_g = \sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\int_\Omega\|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c\|^2\,\mathrm dV} = \sqrt{\sum_{p=1}^{N_q} \tilde w_p \|\mathbf x_p - \mathbf x_c\|^2}, \\
\tilde{\mathbf x} = \frac{\mathbf x - \mathbf x_c}{r_g}.
\label{eq:dimless-coords}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Here the Cartesian coordinate directions correspond to the standard anatomical axes: $x$ is mediolateral (ML), $y$ is anteroposterior (AP), and $z$ is craniocaudal (CC).

In the discrete quadrature representation, each displacement field is a stacked vector $\mathbf U \in \mathbb{R}^{3N_q}$ with components $U_{3p-3+i} = \sqrt{\tilde w_p} \, u_i(\mathbf x_p)$ for $i \in \{1,2,3\}$.
The 6 rigid-body design modes $\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_6 \in \mathbb{R}^{3N_q}$ (corresponding to unit translations along $x,y,z$ and unit infinitesimal rotations about $x,y,z$) form the rigid design matrix $\mathbf R \in \mathbb{R}^{3N_q \times 6}$.
We compute its thin QR decomposition,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf R = \mathbf Q_{\rm rigid} \mathbf R_{\rm upper}, \\
 \mathbf Q_{\rm rigid} \in \mathbb{R}^{3N_q \times 6}, \quad \mathbf Q_{\rm rigid}^\top \mathbf Q_{\rm rigid} = \mathbf I_6.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The orthogonal projection operator onto the nonrigid displacement subspace is then:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf P_{\rm nonrigid} = \mathbf I_{3N_q} - \mathbf Q_{\rm rigid} \mathbf Q_{\rm rigid}^\top.
\label{eq:nonrigid-proj}
\end{equation}
For a sampled modal field $\mathbf U$, define $\mathbf U_{\rm nr}=\mathbf P_{\rm nonrigid}\mathbf U$ and $\mathbf U_{\rm rigid}=\mathbf U-\mathbf U_{\rm nr}$. Because the quadrature weights are incorporated into $\mathbf U$, its Euclidean norm equals the quadrature-evaluated continuum norm. Orthogonality gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\|\mathbf U\|_2^2=\|\mathbf U_{\rm rigid}\|_2^2+\|\mathbf U_{\rm nr}\|_2^2,\qquad\|\mathbf U\|_2^2=\|\mathbf u\|_V^2,\\
f_{\rm nonrigid}=\frac{\|\mathbf U_{\rm nr}\|_2^2}{\|\mathbf U\|_2^2}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The decomposition acts on $\mathbf U_{\rm nr}$ and excludes the projected rigid component. A field with $f_{\rm nonrigid}\le10^{-20}$ is classified as rigid and excluded from the nonrigid decomposition.

\subsubsection{Canonical 3D polynomial dictionary}
The vector space of polynomial displacement fields $\mathbf u(\tilde{\mathbf x})$ of degree at most 2 in three dimensions has complete algebraic dimension $3 \times \binom{3+2}{2} = \TotalPolyDOFs$.
Subtracting the \RigidDOFs\ degrees of freedom of infinitesimal rigid motion leaves an exact \NonrigidDOFs-dimensional nonrigid polynomial quotient space.
We partition this polynomial quotient into four kinematic families with a total of \NonrigidDOFs\ independent degrees of freedom:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Axial family ($d_{\rm axial} = 6$ degrees of freedom):}
Uniform normal extension/compression along each anatomical axis, together with quadratic normal strain gradients:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a_{x,1} &= (\tilde x, 0, 0)^\top, \\
 \mathbf a_{x,2} &= (\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde x^2, 0, 0)^\top, \\
\mathbf a_{y,1} &= (0, \tilde y, 0)^\top, \\
 \mathbf a_{y,2} &= (0, \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde y^2, 0)^\top, \\
\mathbf a_{z,1} &= (0, 0, \tilde z)^\top, \\
 \mathbf a_{z,2} &= (0, 0, \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde z^2)^\top.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:axial-family}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Bending family ($d_{\rm bending} = 6$ degrees of freedom):}
Shear-free quadratic flexural fields of Euler--Bernoulli form.
For any pair of distinct coordinate axes $i \ne j \in \{x, y, z\}$, the displacement field
\begin{equation}
\mathbf b_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde x_i^2 \mathbf e_j - \tilde x_i \tilde x_j \mathbf e_i
\label{eq:bending-family}
\end{equation}
satisfies $\partial_i u_j = \tilde x_i$ and $\partial_j u_i = -\tilde x_i$.
Consequently, the Cauchy shear strain vanishes identically:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{ij}(\mathbf b_{ij}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\partial_j u_i + \partial_i u_j\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(-\tilde x_i + \tilde x_i\big) \equiv 0.
\end{equation}
The six ordered pairs $(x,y)$, $(x,z)$, $(y,x)$, $(y,z)$, $(z,x)$, $(z,y)$ thus define six independent shear-free flexural modes.

\item \textbf{Shear family ($d_{\rm shear} = 10$ degrees of freedom):}
Comprises three uniform transverse shears, six planar quadratic transverse shear gradients, and one triaxial cross-gradient shear field $\mathbf w$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf s_{xy,1} &= (\tilde y, \tilde x, 0)^\top, \\
 \mathbf s_{xy,2} &= (\tilde x \tilde y, \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde x^2, 0)^\top, \\
 \mathbf s_{yx,2} &= (\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde y^2, \tilde y \tilde x, 0)^\top, \\
\mathbf s_{yz,1} &= (0, \tilde z, \tilde y)^\top, \\
 \mathbf s_{yz,2} &= (0, \tilde y \tilde z, \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde y^2)^\top, \\
 \mathbf s_{zy,2} &= (0, \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde z^2, \tilde z \tilde y)^\top, \\
\mathbf s_{zx,1} &= (\tilde z, 0, \tilde x)^\top, \\
 \mathbf s_{zx,2} &= (\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde z^2, 0, \tilde z \tilde x)^\top, \\
 \mathbf s_{xz,2} &= (\tilde x \tilde z, 0, \tfrac{1}{2}\tilde x^2)^\top, \\
\mathbf w &= (\tilde y \tilde z, \tilde x \tilde z, \tilde x \tilde y)^\top = \nabla(\tilde x \tilde y \tilde z).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:shear-family}
\end{equation}
Unlike bending, these modes produce non-zero shear strains $\varepsilon_{ij} \ne 0$.
The triaxial mode $\mathbf w$ is irrotational ($\nabla \times \mathbf w = \mathbf 0$) and divergence-free ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf w = 0$), completing the chosen basis of the \NonrigidDOFs-dimensional nonrigid polynomial quotient.

\item \textbf{Torsion family ($d_{\rm torsion} = 2$ degrees of freedom):}
Polynomial twist-gradient fields around the three Cartesian axes:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf t_x = (0, -\tilde x \tilde z, \tilde x \tilde y)^\top, \quad
\mathbf t_y = (\tilde y \tilde z, 0, -\tilde x \tilde y)^\top, \quad
\mathbf t_z = (-\tilde y \tilde z, \tilde x \tilde z, 0)^\top.
\label{eq:torsion-family}
\end{equation}
Each torsion field exhibits identically zero normal strain ($\varepsilon_{xx} = \varepsilon_{yy} = \varepsilon_{zz} \equiv 0$).
Crucially, these three vector fields satisfy the identity:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf t_x + \mathbf t_y + \mathbf t_z \equiv \mathbf 0.
\end{equation}
Hence, this selected torsion-like polynomial family has algebraic dimension 2, spanned by $\{\mathbf t_x, \mathbf t_y\}$.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:dictionary-families} shows one rigid-projected polynomial field from each family on the reference anatomy.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.64\textheight,keepaspectratio]{deformation_families.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Representative fields of the 3D polynomial deformation dictionary.}
One example per family is evaluated on the reference pelvis: \textbf{(A)} axial extension, $\mathbf a_{x,1}$; \textbf{(B)} shear-free bending, $\mathbf b_{xy}$; \textbf{(C)} uniform transverse shear, $\mathbf s_{xy,1}$; and \textbf{(D)} torsion-like twist, $\mathbf t_x$.
Each card shows anterior and cranial views. Grey denotes the undeformed geometry; colour indicates displacement magnitude relative to the maximum within that field. To assemble the discrete kinematic design matrix, the basis fields in each family $m \in \{\text{axial, bending, shear, torsion}\}$ are sampled at the $N_q$ quadrature points and weighted by $\sqrt{\tilde w_p}$ to form $\mathbf A_m^{\rm raw}$. They are projected orthogonal to $\mathbf Q_{\rm rigid}$ and orthonormalised via reduced singular value decomposition (SVD):
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf P_{\rm nonrigid} \mathbf A_m^{\rm raw} = \mathbf Q_m \mathbf \Sigma_m \mathbf V_m^\top, \\
 \mathbf Q_m \in \mathbb{R}^{3N_q \times d_m}, \quad \mathbf Q_m^\top \mathbf Q_m = \mathbf I_{d_m}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The concatenated design matrix $\mathbf A = [\mathbf Q_{\rm axial}, \mathbf Q_{\rm bending}, \mathbf Q_{\rm shear}, \mathbf Q_{\rm torsion}] \in \mathbb{R}^{3N_q \times \NonrigidDOFs}$ spans the complete \NonrigidDOFs-dimensional canonical nonrigid deformation subspace.

\subsubsection{Numerical conditioning, family non-orthogonality, and the Gram matrix}
The four family subspaces are not mutually orthogonal even on an idealised symmetric domain.
For example, on the symmetric unit cube $[-1, 1]^3$ (volume $V = 8$), the unnormalised continuum volume integral between the bending mode $\mathbf b = (-xy, \frac{1}{2}x^2, 0)^\top$ and the shear mode $\mathbf s = (xy, \frac{1}{2}x^2, 0)^\top$ evaluates analytically to $\int_{[-1, 1]^3} \mathbf b \cdot \mathbf s \, \mathrm dV = -22/45 \approx -0.4889$, corresponding to a volume-normalised inner product $\langle \mathbf b, \mathbf s \rangle_V = \frac{1}{V}\int_{[-1, 1]^3} \mathbf b \cdot \mathbf s \, \mathrm dV = -11/180 \approx -0.0611$ (angular cosine $\cos\theta = -11/29 \approx -0.3793$).
Both example fields have the same nonzero mean translation $(0,1/6,0)^\top$. After subtracting it, their volume-normalised inner product is $-4/45$ and their cosine is $-2/3$. Thus non-orthogonality also holds after the rigid-motion projection used here.

On the real, geometrically asymmetric pelvic ring, this intrinsic non-orthogonality combines with anatomical geometry.
The kinematic design matrix $\mathbf A$ has 2-norm condition number $\kappa(\mathbf A) \approx \DesignConditionNumber$, and the associated Gram matrix $\mathbf G = \mathbf A^\top \mathbf A \in \mathbb{R}^{\NonrigidDOFs \times \NonrigidDOFs}$ has condition number:
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\mathbf G) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf G)}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf G)} = \kappa(\mathbf A)^2 \approx \GramConditionNumber.
\end{equation}
At the reference geometry, the sampled design columns are numerically independent (Gram condition number $\sim21$). Family shares remain conditional on the selected dictionary.

\subsubsection{Cooperative game-theoretic Shapley value norm allocation}
Because the four family subspaces are not mutually orthogonal, sequential projection would allocate correlated fitted norm according to family order. We use Shapley allocation to average marginal contributions over all orders.
To obtain an order-independent allocation conditional on this chosen dictionary, we formulate the problem using cooperative game theory.

Let $M = \{\text{axial}, \text{bending}, \text{shear}, \text{torsion}\}$ denote the set of four pattern players ($|M| = 4$).
There are $2^4 = 16$ possible coalitions $S \subseteq M$.
For any coalition $S$, $\mathbf A_S$ concatenates the orthonormal family matrices $\mathbf Q_m$ for $m\in S$ and has $\sum_{m\in S}d_m$ columns. We define the characteristic function $v(S)$ as the fraction of nonrigid squared displacement norm explained by their joint span:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
v(S) = R^2(S) = \frac{\mathbf U_{\rm nr}^\top \mathbf P_S \mathbf U_{\rm nr}}{\|\mathbf U_{\rm nr}\|_2^2}, \\
\mathbf P_S = \mathbf A_S (\mathbf A_S^\top \mathbf A_S)^+ \mathbf A_S^\top,
\label{eq:coalition-r2}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $v(\emptyset)=0$ and $V_S=\operatorname{range}(\mathbf A_S)$; $(\cdot)^+$ denotes the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse. The value is defined only when $\|\mathbf U_{\rm nr}\|_2>0$.
For a given coalition value function $v$, the allocation $f_m(v)$ satisfies the following axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Efficiency:} The sum of allocated values equals the value of the grand coalition:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{m \in M} f_m = v(M) = R^2(M) = 1 - f_{\rm residual}.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Symmetry:} If two families $m, k \in M$ contribute identically to every coalition ($v(S \cup \{m\}) = v(S \cup \{k\})$ for all $S \subseteq M \setminus \{m, k\}$), then $f_m = f_k$.
\item \textbf{Dummy player:} If a family $m$ adds zero marginal variance to every coalition ($v(S \cup \{m\}) = v(S)$ for all $S \subseteq M \setminus \{m\}$), then $f_m = 0$.
\item \textbf{Additivity:} For any two characteristic value functions $v$ and $w$ defined on the same set of players, the values allocated under the combined sum game $(v + w)(S) = v(S) + w(S)$ satisfy $f_m(v+w)=f_m(v)+f_m(w)$.
\end{enumerate}
The explicit Shapley allocation formula evaluates the marginal contribution of family $m$ averaged over all coalition orders:
\begin{equation}
f_m = \sum_{S \subseteq M \setminus \{m\}} \frac{|S|!(|M| - |S| - 1)!}{|M|!} \Big[ v\big(S \cup \{m\}\big) - v(S) \Big].
\label{eq:shapley-formula}
\end{equation}
The unexplained shape change is retained as an explicit residual:
\begin{equation}
f_{\rm residual} = 1 - v(M) = 1 - R^2(\text{axial} \cup \text{bending} \cup \text{shear} \cup \text{torsion}).
\end{equation}
Because subspace projection operators are monotonic ($V_S \subseteq V_{S \cup \{m\}} \implies v(S \cup \{m\}) \ge v(S)$), every marginal contribution is non-negative, which guarantees $f_m \ge 0$ for all $m$.
Exact closure holds identically:
\begin{equation}
f_{\rm axial} + f_{\rm bending} + f_{\rm shear} + f_{\rm torsion} + f_{\rm residual} \equiv 1 \quad (100\%).
\end{equation}
Furthermore, the fractions are strictly scale-invariant ($\mathbf u \mapsto c\mathbf u$ for $c \ne 0$) and sign-invariant ($\mathbf u \mapsto -\mathbf u$).
For comparative diagnostics, we also compute the standalone explained-norm fraction for each isolated family:
\begin{equation}
R^2_m = v(\{m\}) = \frac{\mathbf U_{\rm nr}^\top \mathbf P_m \mathbf U_{\rm nr}}{\|\mathbf U_{\rm nr}\|_2^2}.
\end{equation}
Because the families are not mutually orthogonal, $\sum_{m \in M} R^2_m$ can exceed the jointly explained fraction $R^2(M)$. The Shapley values allocate shared explanatory content in an order-independent way conditional on the selected dictionary and anatomical axes.

\section{First-order material-parameter sensitivity}\label{app:uq}
\subsection{Methods: first-order eigenvalue sensitivities and analytical uncertainty propagation}
\label{sec:sensitivities}

To propagate material-parameter uncertainty to the spectrum, we use classical first-order sensitivities of the generalised eigenproblem~\eqref{eq:gep}: for a parameter $p_j$ that affects only the stiffness,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\lambda_m}{\partial p_j}
=\frac{\boldsymbol\phi_m^\top\,(\partial K_s/\partial p_j)\,\boldsymbol\phi_m}
{\boldsymbol\phi_m^\top M_s\,\boldsymbol\phi_m}.
\label{eq:ev-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
For ligament parameters, derivatives of local $3\times3$ blocks are $\partial W/\partial k_{\rm seg}=\mathbf e\mathbf e^\top$ and $\partial W_g/\partial T_{\rm seg}=(I-\mathbf e\mathbf e^\top)/L$. They enter the signed $6\times6$ segment assembly, with factor $1/N_{\rm seg}$ for differentiation with respect to a bundle parameter.
For a cartilage region with fixed Poisson ratio, $\partial K_s/\partial E_{\rm region}=K_{\rm region}/E_{\rm region}$, where $K_{\rm region}$ is its assembled baseline stiffness contribution.
At source points on the high-density branch of~\eqref{eq:keller}, $\partial E/\partial\alpha=\rho_{\rm ash}^{\beta}$ and $\partial E/\partial\beta=\alpha\rho_{\rm ash}^{\beta}\ln\rho_{\rm ash}$; the derivatives vanish on the constant branch. We interpolate each source derivative with the same radial-basis-function operator used for the modulus, then integrate it against the modal strain-energy density. This differentiates the implemented map, in which the nonlinear density law precedes interpolation. A finite-difference check at 1669 sampled cells in one subject agreed to relative $L^2$ errors below $4\times10^{-11}$ for both parameters.
For each positive material parameter $p_j$, let $p_j^0$ denote its baseline value and $\xi_j=\log(p_j/p_j^0)$. For each simple eigenvalue, we store the dimensionless logarithmic sensitivity
\begin{equation}
D_j^{(m)}=\frac{p_j^0}{\lambda_m^0}\left.\frac{\partial\lambda_m}{\partial p_j}\right|_{\mathbf p=\mathbf p^0}=\left.\frac{\partial\log(\lambda_m/\lambda_m^0)}{\partial\xi_j}\right|_{\boldsymbol\xi=0}.
\end{equation}
With $\mathbf D_m=(D_j^{(m)})_j$ and $\Sigma_{\xi}=\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol\xi)$, first-order propagation gives $\mathrm{Var}[\log(\lambda_m/\lambda_m^0)]\approx\mathbf D_m^\top\Sigma_{\xi}\mathbf D_m$ under the assumed material-parameter covariance. The implemented scenarios use log-space standard deviations $(0.25,0.20,0.10)$ for global, type, and side effects of ligament stiffness; $(0.35,0.25,0.12)$ for ligament pretension; and $(0.35,0.25,0.10)$ for cartilage modulus. The global ligament stiffness--pretension correlation is 0.50. Bone-law parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ have log-space standard deviations 0.30 and 0.20 with correlation $-0.40$. These covariance scenarios were prescribed for the sensitivity analysis. Eigenvalue CoVs use the lognormal conversion $\sqrt{\exp(v)-1}$ from propagated log-variance $v$. In this appendix, the superscript $0$ denotes baseline-parameter eigenvalues, not the shared reference mass matrix $M_{\rm ref}^0$.
For adjacent eigenvalues $\lambda_i, \lambda_{i+1}$, the variance of the gap $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i$ is computed from the combined sensitivity vector $\mathbf g_{i,i+1} = \lambda_{i+1}^0\mathbf D_{i+1} - \lambda_i^0\mathbf D_i$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i) \approx \mathbf g_{i,i+1}^\top \Sigma_{\xi}\,\mathbf g_{i,i+1} \approx \mathrm{Var}(\lambda_{i+1}) + \mathrm{Var}(\lambda_i) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\lambda_i, \lambda_{i+1}),
\label{eq:gap-cov}
\end{equation}
accounting for the substantial positive covariance induced by shared tissue parameters.
The gap signal-to-noise ratio compares the nominal gap with its propagated standard deviation (Section~\ref{sec:uq-results}). It measures local susceptibility of modal ordering under the specified parameter covariance. The propagated quantities are eigenvalues and adjacent gaps. Sex-stratified results appear below.

Under this assumed local perturbation model, the internal 9--10 ordering is therefore more susceptible than the low-rank gaps. \section{Analytical example of close eigenvalues}\label{app:toy}
\subsection{Methods: analytical model for the near-degenerate regime}
\label{sec:toy-methods}

The cluster/subspace framework admits a compact analytical interpretation via a two-degree-of-freedom model.
Let $z_1,z_2$ denote two generalised coordinates and define a stiffness matrix
\begin{equation}
K_2=\begin{bmatrix} k_1 & c\\ c & k_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Delta=k_1-k_2,\qquad k_1,k_2>0,\ k_1k_2>c^2,
\end{equation}
with detuning~$\Delta$ and coupling~$c$.
The eigenvalues are
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}\pm\sqrt{\Big(\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Big)^2+c^2},
\qquad
\tan(2\theta)=\frac{2c}{\Delta},
\label{eq:2dof-eig}
\end{equation}
and the minimum separation $\lambda_+-\lambda_-=2|c|$ is attained at $\Delta=0$, for constant nonzero $c$ as $\Delta$ is varied. With a continuous branch convention, $2\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(2c,\Delta)$; at $\Delta=0$ the angle is $\pi/4$ for $c>0$, up to eigenvector sign. When $c=0$, an exact crossing occurs.
The operational criterion
\begin{equation}
|\Delta(\mu)|\le\tau\,|c(\mu)|\quad\text{for some morphology parameter }\mu,\qquad \tau\in[1.5,2],
\label{eq:2dof-criterion}
\end{equation}
is an illustrative mixing criterion for the reduced model; it is distinct from the cohort cluster threshold in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap-in}.
Adding a third coordinate $z_3$ with stiffness~$k_3$ and coupling vector $\mathbf d = [d_1, d_2]^\top$, dynamic reduction of the $3\times 3$ eigenproblem $(K - \lambda I)\mathbf z = \mathbf 0$ gives the exact non-linear reduced eigenproblem
\begin{equation}
\Big( K_2 - \frac{\mathbf d \mathbf d^\top}{k_3 - \lambda} \Big) \mathbf z_{12} = \lambda \mathbf z_{12}.
\label{eq:dynamic-schur}
\end{equation}
In the low-frequency limit ($\lambda \ll k_3$), static condensation yields the linear effective stiffness
\begin{equation}
K_{\rm eff} = K_2 - \frac{\mathbf d \mathbf d^\top}{k_3} = \begin{bmatrix} k_1-d_1^2/k_3 & c-d_1d_2/k_3\\ c-d_1d_2/k_3 & k_2-d_2^2/k_3\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:schur}
\end{equation}
showing how coupling to an additional coordinate shifts effective detuning and coupling. Depending on the parameters, the shift can move the system toward or away from avoided crossing. The assumption $\lambda\ll k_3$ excludes using this condensation for an arbitrarily soft hidden coordinate.
All stochastic simulations solve the full $3\times 3$ eigenproblem (Section~\ref{sec:toy-results}).
The two-coordinate span is an analogue of an FE modal cluster, and the two-coordinate model relative gap is $(\lambda_+-\lambda_-)/\lambda_-$. The illustrative eigenvector rotations are not identified with the anatomical-capacity optimisation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-functional-max-nodal}.
A stochastic instantiation, evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:toy-results}, illustrates rank redistribution, paired projection, and load routing under the chosen model parameters.
Section~\ref{sec:toy-specification} specifies the stochastic model used in the figure.

\subsubsection{Stochastic implementation}\label{sec:toy-specification}
The stochastic implementation evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:toy-results} uses dimensionless illustrative parameters. The stiffness matrix is
\begin{equation}
K=\begin{bmatrix}12+\Delta/2&c&d_1\\c&12-\Delta/2&d_2\\d_1&d_2&k_3\end{bmatrix},\qquad M=I.
\end{equation}
For morphology coordinate $\mu$ and nuisance scale $\eta$, independent standard-normal draws determine
\begin{align}
\Delta&=1.6\mu+(0.05+0.22\eta)Z_\Delta,\\
c&=|0.22+(0.008+0.018\eta)Z_c|,\\
k_3&=5.2\exp[(0.02+0.12\eta)Z_3],\\
d_1&=0.28+0.42\eta+(0.01+0.08\eta)Z_1,\\
d_2&=0.24+0.40\eta+(0.01+0.08\eta)Z_2.
\end{align}
Draws are accepted only when all eigenvalues exceed $10^{-10}$ and the largest absolute component of the third coordinate occurs in the lowest eigenvector. This acceptance criterion retains a low-rank backbone by construction. Up to 200 draws are attempted per subject.

The anatomical observable and task-like load are unit vectors proportional to $(1,0,0.2)^\top$; the standing-like load is a unit vector proportional to $(0.10,0.05,1)^\top$. Writing $\mathbf b$ for the unit observable and $\mathbf f$ for a unit load, the observable projection of a unit eigenvector $\mathbf v_i$ is $(\mathbf v_i^\top\mathbf b)^2$ and the upper-pair score is the sum for $i=2,3$. Load fractions are proportional to $(\mathbf v_i^\top\mathbf f)^2/\lambda_i$. The analytical example uses squared projections as illustrative observables; the pelvic cohort uses max-nodal anatomical capacity.

The main illustrative cohort contains 240 evenly spaced coordinates in $[-1.10,1.10]$, perturbed by normal noise of standard deviation 0.035 (random seed 11), sorted, and simulated at $\eta=0.85$ (seed 19). The nuisance sweep uses nine values in $[0,1.6]$ and 480 coordinates in $[-0.35,0.35]$, with seed 31 at each value. Carrier change is the proportion whose larger upper-pair observable projection changes rank relative to $\eta=0$. Pair projection and task routing are summarised by their medians.

For every accepted draw, the full $3\times3$ eigenproblem gives the modal ordering and observable projections. The static-condensation formula in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} provides the limiting interpretation for $\lambda\ll k_3$.


\subsection{Results: label--subspace dissociation}
\label{sec:toy-results}

The constructed analytical example illustrates how a separated low-rank mode and a coupled higher pair can produce contrasting rank and subspace behaviour (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}). As detuning varies, the dominant projection of the anatomical observable transfers between the upper eigenvectors, whereas their summed projection remains high. Standing-like and task-like loads recruit different spectral regions. Increasing the nuisance scale changes the dominant carrier more strongly than the upper-pair projection or task routing.

The analytical example conditions accepted draws on a retained low-rank mode and measures coupling by squared projection.
