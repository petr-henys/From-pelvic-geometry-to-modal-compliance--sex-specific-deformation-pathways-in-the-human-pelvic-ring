\section{Introduction}
The sacroiliac joint (SIJ) is a mechanically demanding articulation of the pelvic ring: it must transmit loads between the trunk and lower limbs while permitting only small, tightly constrained motions \citep{Vleeming2012,Snijders1993}. That combination---effective load transfer with low mobility---is enabled by irregular articular surfaces, strong ligamentous restraint, and the closed-ring organization of the pelvis \citep{Vleeming2012,Snijders1993}. More broadly, the human pelvis sits at the intersection of locomotor and obstetric demands, a framing that continues to motivate discussions of pelvic form and function in anatomy, anthropology, and biomechanics \citep{Washburn1960,Whitcome2017,Gruss2015,Ruff2017}.

Because these motions are small, they are difficult to measure consistently in vivo and across experimental setups. Reported SIJ kinematics vary with both task and measurement method, and the literature emphasizes that the joint should be treated as a low-motion but load-sensitive structure rather than a rigid articulation \citep{Goode2008,Meijer2014,Keizer2019,Noe2020,Vleeming2012}. Crucially, SIJ motion should not be expected to scale uniformly across directions: translation components in the anteroposterior (AP), mediolateral (ML), and craniocaudal (CC) axes can change disproportionately with loading direction and constraint, reflecting anisotropic joint anatomy and the ligamentous network of the pelvic ring \citep{Vleeming2012,Goode2008,Meijer2014,Noe2020}.

Directional behavior becomes most informative when loading changes. Unilateral support in locomotion increases asymmetry compared with quiet two-leg standing, and instrumented studies show that routine activities can generate large, task-dependent hip forces that must be routed through the pelvic ring \citep{Bergmann2001JBiomech,Kutzner2016PLOS}. In obstetric contexts, pelvic anatomy and loading are additionally shaped by parturition-related functional demands, including uterine contraction and intra-abdominal pressure environments that differ across pregnancy and labor \citep{AJOG2022Uterine,Fuchs2013PLOS,Chun2012IAPReview,BiomechPregnancy2022}. Pelvic form varies substantially between individuals and between sexes, including dimensions that relate to inlet, midplane, and outlet architecture \citep{Ruff2017,Betti2020,Weber2019,Whitcome2017,Weiss2020}. Such variation can shift lever arms, articular orientation, and ligament attachment geometry, and therefore may alter how otherwise standardized external forces are redistributed into SIJ micromotion. Beyond sex, skeletal tissues remodel across the lifespan, suggesting that age-related changes in geometry and material behavior may further modulate joint-level response \citep{FarrKhosla2015,Zebaze2010Porosity}.

	Finite-element (FE) modelling provides a complementary route to SIJ biomechanics because it can combine subject-specific anatomy with controlled boundary conditions and precisely defined load cases. Prior SIJ and pelvic-ring FE studies show substantial sensitivity to ligament restraint, cartilage properties, and loading configuration \citep{Anderson2020,Yang2020,Song2021,Calixte2022}. Computational childbirth models likewise highlight both the promise and the complexity of obstetric pelvic mechanics, including the interplay of geometry, tissue properties, and load direction, but many models are single-subject or focus on contact-rich scenarios rather than population-scale joint micromotion \citep{ChenGrimm2021,Almeida2024Uterus,BiomechPregnancy2022}. More generally, SIJ FE studies are often limited by cohort size, by geometric normalization that removes absolute size information, or by uncertainty about whether between-subject dispersion is driven primarily by geometry (shape) versus material mapping.

	Increasingly, large-cohort FE workflows adopt a common reference domain and encode subject-specific anatomy through an implicit mapping rather than explicit per-subject remeshing. This representation improves computational scalability, but it also shifts how ``geometry'' is represented and how size-related covariates should be interpreted: individual shape is carried by the mapping, even when the computational mesh is shared.

	Two gaps are especially important for interpreting cohort-scale SIJ micromotion. First, variance in predicted motion can arise from at least two mechanistic ``channels'': geometric variability (including joint orientation and pelvic ring architecture) and material variability (e.g., density-based bone stiffness mapping) \citep{CarterHayes1977,Keller1994,Morgan2003Trabecular,Frost1987,Huiskes1987}. Distinguishing these channels is necessary to determine whether anatomy alone largely governs \emph{between-subject variability} under standardized loads, or whether plausible material heterogeneity contributes comparably. Second, size scaling of SIJ micromotion remains underreported in large cohorts. Under force-standardized loading, larger load-bearing geometry may reduce micromotion, and global size can confound sex comparisons given well-established sex-related pelvic shape differences \citep{Ruff2017,Betti2020,Weber2019,Weiss2020}. In reference-domain workflows, this requires a consistent definition of ``size'' derived from the same implicit geometry representation as the FE solve.

	To address these issues, we analyzed a large precomputed FE repository of 278 pelves in which subject geometry is represented on a common template through an implicit displacement mapping $\phi$. The repository includes full, shape-only, and material-only variants, enabling variance-channel decomposition under identical standardized load definitions. We evaluated SIJ rotations and translations under five static load cases spanning locomotor and parturition-motivated proxies (two-leg standing, one-leg standing, and three labor-motivated loading states). By combining directional (AP/ML/CC) outcomes with robust and cluster-robust regression, we aimed to quantify load-dependent directional re-routing, sex effects under parturition-motivated loading after coarse anatomical adjustment, the relative contribution of shape versus material variance channels, and power-law size scaling of SIJ micromotion using total pelvic volume as a global proxy. We therefore tested the following pre-specified hypotheses.

\subsection*{Hypotheses}
We pre-specified four mechanistic hypotheses, each phrased as an explicit, testable prediction:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{H1 (load dependence and re-routing):} under identical standardized resultant forces, SIJ micromotion exhibits load-case-specific patterns. Compared with bilateral standing, unilateral standing increases overall motion magnitude, whereas parturition-motivated loading produces non-uniform changes across AP/ML/CC translation components (directional re-routing) rather than uniform scaling of all components.
\item \textbf{H2 (sex effects conditional on anatomy):} under parturition-motivated loads, female sex is associated with greater SIJ micromotion after adjustment for age, a global size proxy, and a parsimonious set of pelvic dimensions chosen to capture inlet depth, outlet breadth, and pubic-arch opening.
\item \textbf{H3 (geometry-dominant variability):} between-subject variability in SIJ outcomes is dominated by geometric differences: shape-only simulations recover most of the full-model variance, whereas material-only perturbations explain a comparatively small fraction under the standardized loading protocol.
\item \textbf{H4 (size scaling):} SIJ micromotion follows load-dependent power-law scaling with global pelvic size. Under force-standardized standing loads, negative scaling is expected to be most evident in higher-demand support conditions, and potential sex differences in scaling are evaluated via an interaction term rather than assumed a priori.
\end{enumerate}
