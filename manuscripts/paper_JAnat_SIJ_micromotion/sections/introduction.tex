\section{Introduction}
The sacroiliac joint (SIJ) transfers load between the trunk and lower limbs while allowing small relative movements of the sacrum and ilia. Articular geometry and ligamentous restraint contribute to this function; muscular compression also contributes in vivo \citep{Vleeming2012,Snijders1993,Hammer2013}. The relationship between pelvic stability and obstetric accommodation motivates questions about pelvic mechanics, but a static skeletal model cannot by itself establish an evolutionary trade-off or predict childbirth outcomes \citep{Pavlicev2020,ChenGrimm2021}.

Population-scale pelvic finite-element (FE) modeling is already established. In particular, \citet{HenysHammer2025} analyzed 281 pelvic reconstructions and related SIJ morphology to its mechanical environment. The present study therefore does not claim novelty for cohort size or for the general proposition that geometry matters. Its intended contribution is a within-subject comparison of five standardized load configurations, with separate absolute motion components and matched geometry/material variants. This design asks whether changing load location and direction changes the distribution of joint motion across anatomical axes, and whether these responses covary with sex and pelvic dimensions.

A second question concerns the extent to which a reference bone-density field can reproduce subject-specific kinematic responses. This is a restricted modeling question: holding cartilage and ligament properties fixed does not test their population variability. Recent work identifies ligament pretension as an influential SIJ modeling parameter \citep{Heyland2025}. Thus a comparison between geometry and bone-density variants cannot establish that geometry dominates all tissue properties.

We analyzed 278 imaging-derived pelvic models (150 female and 128 male; age 16--91 years) from the standardized BoneDat workflow \citep{HenyssKuchar2025BoneDat}. Two standing configurations and three localized, parturition-motivated force pairs were compared at identical prescribed forces across subjects. The latter are mechanical probes rather than successive stages of labor. Joint motion was measured relative to each subject's unloaded mapped anatomy, separating elastic displacement from inter-individual shape differences.

\subsection*{Hypotheses and scope}
The following hypotheses organize a retrospective analysis; they were not preregistered and should not be interpreted as independent prospective confirmation.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{H1 (standing asymmetry):} Unilateral loading increases bilateral translation-magnitude asymmetry and overall joint motion relative to bilateral loading.
\item \textbf{H2 (component redistribution):} Relative to bilateral standing, LAB1 and LAB2 reduce the absolute CC translation component and increase the absolute ML component. LAB3 is assessed separately rather than assumed to share the same response. Absolute components do not establish signed joint opening or nutation direction.
\item \textbf{H3 (conditional sex association):} Female models have greater translation under the three LAB configurations after age adjustment; adjustment for size and the morphometric triad reduces this difference. These are conditional associations, not a causal mediation model.
\item \textbf{H4 (restricted material simplification):} Shape-only responses retain most of the full-model between-subject variance and agree with individual full-model predictions more closely than the material-only variant suggests. Variance ratios are not additive causal contributions.
\end{enumerate}
Volume--motion associations and sex-by-volume interactions were exploratory secondary analyses. Their exponents describe conditional associations under fixed applied force, not universal physiological scaling laws.
