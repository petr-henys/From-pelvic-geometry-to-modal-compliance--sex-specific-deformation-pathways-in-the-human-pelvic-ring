\section{Introduction}
Small sacroiliac joint (SIJ) movements provide a way to examine how the pelvic ring transfers load. Articular geometry, ligamentous restraint and muscular compression contribute to stability, while the distribution of force determines which parts of the ring carry that load \citep{Vleeming2012,Snijders1993,Hammer2013}. Describing joint motion therefore requires both an anatomical description and a defined mechanical context.

Human pelvic architecture varies in overall size and in the proportions of the inlet, midpelvis and pubic arch. Sex captures some of this variation, including developmental differences in obstetrically relevant dimensions \citep{Huseynov2016}, but combines several anatomical features into one category. A female--male difference in joint response could reflect pelvic size, inlet depth, interspinous width or subpubic geometry, as well as features not represented by those measurements. A group contrast alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

The central anatomical question is whether sex retains an association with SIJ response once these dimensions are described explicitly. Comparing age-adjusted and anatomy-adjusted sex coefficients can establish how much the group contrast changes, without identifying a causal pathway. This distinction matters for functional interpretations of pelvic sexual dimorphism: the relationship between support and obstetric accommodation cannot be inferred from an unadjusted mobility difference \citep{Pavlicev2020}.

A complementary question concerns the anatomical information needed to retain individual mechanical responses. Previous population modeling linked SIJ auricular morphology to its mechanical environment \citep{HenysHammer2025}. Matched models that retain individual geometry while standardizing the bone-density field test whether that geometry also preserves joint-motion outputs. The reciprocal comparison, retaining individual density on a common geometry, provides a second reference. These comparisons address the represented bone-density variation, with soft-tissue properties held fixed.

Both questions require comparison of the same anatomies across load paths. Bilateral and unilateral acetabular loading redistribute a fixed total force, while localized force pairs probe different sites on the ring. The latter are motivated by obstetric anatomy but do not simulate childbirth, which additionally involves fetal contact, soft tissues and changing boundary conditions \citep{ChenGrimm2021}. Here, using 278 imaging-derived pelves from the BoneDat workflow \citep{HenyssKuchar2025BoneDat}, our primary aim was to determine how explicit pelvic dimensions alter sex-associated translation differences under localized loading. We then assessed how well individual geometry preserves responses under standardized bone density, using matched load comparisons to establish their mechanical context. Volume--motion scaling was a supporting, exploratory analysis. These questions and their directional hypotheses were retrospective and not preregistered.
