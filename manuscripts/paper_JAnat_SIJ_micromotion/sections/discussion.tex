\section{Discussion}
Explicit pelvic architecture provides a more informative description of modeled SIJ response than sex alone, and that response depends on how the ring is loaded. Under localized loading, including pelvic size and dimensions substantially attenuated the age-adjusted female translation coefficients. A separate comparison showed that preserving individual geometry under a standardized bone-density field retained much of the subject-specific response. Together, these results connect the interpretation of sex contrasts to the anatomical information required by the model.

\subsection*{Why sex is an incomplete mechanical descriptor}
The female and male groups differed in several dimensions: female pelves had lower total bone volume but greater mean inlet depth, interspinous width and subpubic angle (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). Sex therefore summarizes a combination of size and proportions rather than one mechanical feature. Adding those dimensions reduced the female translation coefficients from +0.055 to +0.258~mm across localized loads to -0.031 to +0.013~mm. All fully adjusted translation intervals included zero. This supports interpreting the initial group contrasts in relation to explicit anatomy; it neither establishes mechanical equivalence between sexes nor demonstrates causal mediation by the selected dimensions.

The subpubic-angle comparison exposes a related difficulty with aggregate anatomical associations. The positive pooled correlation with LAB1 translation was not reproduced within either sex: both within-sex estimates were weakly negative and their tests did not reject zero correlation. Thus the pooled pattern cannot be treated as evidence that increasing the angle predicts increasing motion within women or men. These exploratory results favor describing dimensions jointly and examining group structure. The selected triad is not a complete representation of pelvic architecture, and coefficient attenuation alone does not demonstrate improved prediction in new individuals. External assessment should examine anatomical overlap between sexes, collinearity and performance beyond this cohort.

\subsection*{Geometry retains the subject-specific mechanical response}
The matched geometry--density comparison asks a different question from the sex models: how much of an individual's modeled response survives a controlled simplification? Retaining individual geometry with a spatially varying population-median density field gave translation RMSE no greater than 0.028~mm and a minimum identity-line $R^2$ of 0.946. Rotation agreement was weaker but still close, with minimum $R^2=0.878$. The near-100\% shape/full variance ratios and small material-only/full ratios were consistent with these paired comparisons. Individual geometry may therefore be a useful priority when constructing models for these particular joint-motion endpoints; acceptable density simplification depends on the intended error tolerance.

This hierarchy is conditional on the passive static model and its implemented density--modulus law, including a low-density modulus floor. Cartilage properties, ligament properties and pretension were fixed. Ligament pretension can substantially affect SIJ predictions \citep{Heyland2025}, and introducing individual soft-tissue variation could change the relative contributions. The variance ratios compare distinct mechanical systems and do not partition causally explained variance. The practical result is a tested simplification of the represented bone-density field, not evidence that material properties generally have little mechanical relevance.

\subsection*{Pelvic mobility depends on the load path}
Anatomy did not determine a single, context-independent mobility value. Redistributing 800~N from bilateral to unilateral acetabular support increased translation and rotation and strongly amplified bilateral asymmetry. The total applied force was unchanged, but its moments and route through the ring changed. This gives a population-level mechanical context to established accounts of SIJ load transfer \citep{Snijders1993,Vleeming2012}.

Application site was also consequential. Both transverse force pairs reduced the craniocaudal component relative to bilateral support, yet only the ischial pair increased the mediolateral component after multiplicity correction. The complete shared transverse-redistribution hypothesis was not supported. Its failure shows why a common global loading direction is insufficient to specify the joint response. Anatomical dimensions should therefore be interpreted together with the location and distribution of force, rather than used to assign an intrinsic scalar mobility to a pelvis.

\subsection*{Size provides a common scaling context}
The consistently negative conditional volume slopes provide supporting evidence that size influences response magnitude across load paths. Their direction is partly compatible with dimensional expectations: geometrically similar linear-elastic solids under fixed force and modulus give $u\propto L^{-1}$ and $\theta\sim u/L\propto L^{-2}$, corresponding to volume exponents of $-1/3$ and $-2/3$. Fixed spring parameters, heterogeneous tissues and varying proportions prevent these benchmarks from being exact predictions here. The fitted slopes also condition on other pelvic dimensions; they describe residual size associations under prescribed loading, not a universal biological scaling law. Undetected sex-by-volume interactions do not demonstrate equal slopes.

\subsection*{Implications for functional anatomy}
Categorical sex labels describe population anatomy, but explicit dimensions connect that description more directly to a specified mechanical response. Beyond previous work on SIJ auricular morphology \citep{HenysHammer2025}, the present comparisons link whole-pelvis dimensions to sex-associated motion differences and test whether individual responses persist when bone density is standardized. Their functional implication is that pelvic form should be evaluated through its interaction with load path: the same architecture can express different component patterns under different demands.

This interpretation offers a structural question for obstetric anatomy without treating the localized probes as childbirth simulations. Neither absolute mediolateral motion nor the subpubic-angle association measures signed joint opening or birth-canal expansion. Establishing those quantities would require direct surface-separation and canal measurements, alongside the fetal and soft-tissue interactions represented in childbirth models \citep{ChenGrimm2021}. No delivery outcome or evolutionary trade-off follows from the present contrasts.

\subsection*{Limitations and experimental tests}
The model represents passive, static mechanics with a constrained sacrum, nominal cartilage properties and linearized ligaments, without muscle activation or frictional contact. The density comparison inherits these choices. The cohort spans ages 16--91 and does not specifically represent pregnant or laboring women; pregnancy-related adaptation, parity, pain and delivery outcomes were not analyzed. The retrospective selection of dimensions and comparisons also limits confirmatory interpretation.

Affine sacral correction removes sacral strain as well as rigid drift. The six-subject rigid-only sensitivity check gave small changes in the reported endpoints, but cannot establish a cohort-wide error bound or validate their physiological magnitude. Sampling intervals exclude constitutive, mapping and boundary-condition uncertainty. Direct validation against cortical strains has been demonstrated for a subject-specific pelvic model \citep{Anderson2005}; validation of that endpoint cannot be transferred to SIJ kinematics here. Mesh and mapping-quality assessment and independent measurements under matched loads remain necessary, particularly given the difficulty of measuring small SIJ motions \citep{Goode2008}.

The resulting experimental predictions are specific: unilateral loading should increase bilateral asymmetry at matched total acetabular force, and transverse forces at the internal ring and ischial tuberosities should produce different component responses. Tests should measure signed SIJ surface separation alongside the reported point-motion endpoints. Comparing subject-specific and standardized density assignments against those measurements would test the proposed simplification, while introducing measured cartilage, ligament and pretension variation would test its scope. Independent specimens and loading configurations are needed to determine how far the anatomical relationships generalize.
