\section{Discussion}
A small anatomical description predicted a substantial part of the modeled variation in SIJ motion, but its usefulness depended on loading and on what was being predicted. Adding inlet AP diameter, interspinous width and subpubic angle to pelvic volume and age reduced held-out translation RMSE by 8--31\% across five loads. Rotation gains were less uniform, with little additional information for the ischial force pair. A separate controlled redistribution experiment identified a mechanism limiting the combination of scalar responses: direction was lost when endpoint displacement vectors were reduced to magnitudes. Keeping that direction nearly recovered the re-extracted mixed-load motion. These findings distinguish prediction in new subjects under a specified load from transfer to a new combination of force application sites.

\subsection*{Predictive information beyond pelvic size}
The triad represents complementary aspects of the ring: inlet depth, midpelvic width and anterior arch geometry. Its improvement over an age-and-volume model shows that size alone does not capture the predictive information in these dimensions. Internal prediction $R^2$ reached 0.717 for bilateral-support translation and 0.691 for AP-pair rotation, while remaining near 0.31 for ischial-pair translation. No single level of anatomical sufficiency therefore applies to all load--endpoint combinations. Separate models were trained for each load; performance across the five represented loads does not establish prediction in an unseen loading configuration.

Adding all eight measures gave no clear further gain in the paired intervals, and sex terms did not consistently improve prediction after the triad. This suggests that a compact description may be useful within the represented cohort, but does not establish an optimal or universally sufficient set of measurements. All eight variables are broad dimensions; additional local auricular geometry, joint orientation or ligament attachment measures could carry information that these benchmarks do not contain. Such extensions require independent validation. The present error estimates also condition on a retrospectively selected triad: although each regression was fitted without its held-out subjects, validation did not repeat the original anatomical selection. Performance may therefore be optimistic relative to selecting predictors anew or applying them externally.

The contribution extends a previous population morphology study rather than introducing a new anatomical cohort. The current subjects are a subset of the CT collection used by \citet{HenysHammer2025} to study auricular morphology, cartilage and mechanical response. Here relative joint-motion endpoints, paired held-out prediction gains and a controlled load-mixture test quantify the information retained and lost in a reduced description of function. The theoretical contribution lies in this biologically specified test of response reduction; the underlying linear-superposition and vector-norm identities are established mechanics.

\subsection*{A directional boundary to scalar load transfer}
The controlled experiment held individual geometry and tissue properties constant while moving the same transverse force between two sites. Exact superposition of the displacement fields provided the corresponding mixed-force solution, including the common pretension once. At equal force redistribution, a scalar combination overestimated translation sufficiently to give a median within-subject reduction of 21.9\% relative to that combination. A signed-vector combination had approximately 270-fold smaller RMSE. The residual vector error reflects the nonlinear extraction steps, including affine correction and fitted rigid motion, rather than a failure of FE field superposition.

This result has a specific interpretation. Under linear mechanics, responses from different sites remain composable at the field level, but endpoint magnitudes are not sufficient statistics for that composition. Their vectors need not align; the cross-term depends on alignment and cannot be recovered from their norms alone. Thus even perfect subject-specific scalar endpoint estimates cannot generally specify the magnitude under redistributed loading. A transferable reduced model should retain directional response information or explicitly represent the load distribution. This applies the known norm identity to the mechanically constrained pelvic ring and quantifies its consequence for this SIJ endpoint.

The test does not show that directional mismatch causes the held-out errors of the anatomical triad. Those errors concern variation among subjects under each fixed load, whereas the intervention concerns variation in loading within each subject. Nor does it identify which anatomical features determine the endpoint-vector orientations. Linking local anatomy to those directions is a further mechanistic question. Directional endpoint information is supplied by the individual FE models here, so the small vector error is an information diagnostic rather than the performance of an anatomy-only surrogate.

\subsection*{Sex-associated response and anatomical adjustment}
Sex groups differed in both size and proportions (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}), consistent with anatomical evidence separating allometric proportions from pubic-arch dimorphism \citep{Fischer2017} and local auricular differences \citep{Nishi2020}. In the present model, sex is not a separate constitutive input: individualized geometry and density carry the group variation, while soft-tissue properties are shared. Attenuation of the sex coefficient after anatomical adjustment is therefore expected to some degree. The useful additional evidence is the improvement of held-out prediction when the triad is added to size, and the lack of consistent further gain from the group label.

All fully adjusted localized-load sex intervals included zero; this does not demonstrate equivalence, mediation or the absence of biological sex effects. Pooled association models use different specifications and can retain small sex-related coefficients (Supplementary Table S5), without establishing an incremental predictive benefit. The pooled positive subpubic-angle association was also not reproduced within either sex. Group covariance, collinearity and model specification must therefore be considered when interpreting anatomical coefficients. Pregnancy-associated soft-tissue variation and other unrepresented differences cannot be inferred from these simulations.

\subsection*{Geometry, density and the loading context}
Preserving individual geometry with a standardized spatial density field retained the full-model motion much more closely than a limited set of dimensions did. Translation RMSE was at most 0.0280~mm, but individual error reached 0.2195~mm under bilateral support. Geometry-preserving simplification can therefore be useful for these endpoints at a specified error tolerance. It retains both size and complete modeled shape; the comparison does not isolate architecture independently of scale. The variance ratios are non-additive and cannot be interpreted as causal fractions attributable to geometry or density.

This result depends on the implemented density--modulus law, including its low-density floor, and on fixed cartilage, ligament and pretension properties. Subchondral mineralization studies provide anatomical context \citep{Poilliot2023}, while the sensitivity of SIJ models to pretension \citep{Heyland2025} indicates why individualized soft tissues could alter the comparison. The hierarchy observed here is specific to the modeled population and motion endpoints.

Force distribution likewise matters beyond total force. Unilateral acetabular support amplified motion and bilateral asymmetry, and direct internal-ring versus ischial comparisons showed distinct component patterns. These results connect population variation to established accounts of SIJ load transfer \citep{Snijders1993,Vleeming2012}. Exploratory negative volume coefficients support size as a useful covariate, but change with adjustment for the triad. Comparative FE interpretation requires explicit control of size and loading \citep{Dumont2009}; conditional regression at fixed anatomical dimensions is not uniform geometric scaling. The dimensional reference exponents are retained only as supplementary context.

\subsection*{Scope and further tests}
The analyses concern passive static mechanics with a constrained sacrum, nominal soft-tissue properties, linearized ligaments and no muscle activation or frictional contact. Absolute endpoints use the anatomical zero-displacement reference, so they include prescribed pretension. The cohort spans ages 16--91 and does not specifically represent pregnant or laboring women. SIJ point displacement is not articular gapping or birth-canal expansion; childbirth questions require signed surface separation, canal measurements and maternal--fetal contact mechanics \citep{ChenGrimm2021}.

Internal statistical validation is distinct from validation against physical SIJ motion. Sampling intervals omit constitutive, interpolation and boundary-condition uncertainty. The affine correction removes sacral strain as well as drift; a rigid-only check showed small differences in six subjects, with limited cohort coverage. Earlier pelvic FE work validated cortical strains \citep{Anderson2005}, while small SIJ motions remain difficult to measure \citep{Goode2008}; the present cohort has no matched experimental kinematic validation.

The next tests follow directly from the reduction question. An independent cohort should evaluate the fixed size and triad models under matched loads and assess the calibration of individual predictions. Matched specimens should test whether redistributing transverse forces changes signed joint-displacement directions and produces the predicted reduction relative to scalar endpoint combination. A reduced model predicting those directions from anatomy would then test whether the mechanism can be incorporated into transferable anatomical prediction. Measured cartilage, ligament and pretension variation would establish how far the current simplification survives beyond its fixed tissue assumptions.
