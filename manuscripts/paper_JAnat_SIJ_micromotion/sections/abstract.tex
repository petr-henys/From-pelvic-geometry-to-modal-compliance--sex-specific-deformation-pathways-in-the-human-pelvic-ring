Pelvic stability depends on transmitting load through joints that permit only small movements. How does anatomical variation shape these movements when the load path changes? We combined imaging-derived anatomy from 278 pelves (150 female, 128 male) with finite-element analysis of five standardized loads and matched geometry--bone-density variants. Redistributing the same 800~N acetabular force from bilateral to unilateral support increased median sacroiliac translation from 1.058 to 1.627~mm and bilateral translation asymmetry from 0.065 to 1.602~mm. Three localized force pairs produced distinct motion-component patterns, rather than a common increase in transverse motion. Positive age-adjusted female translation differences attenuated after including pelvic size and dimensions; fully adjusted coefficients ranged from -0.031 to +0.013~mm, with all confidence intervals spanning zero. All 20 sex-specific volume slopes were negative after multiplicity correction. Retaining individual geometry with a population-reference bone-density field reproduced full-model translation with RMSE no greater than 0.028~mm across loads. These controlled comparisons show how pelvic architecture and load distribution jointly shape sacroiliac motion, and why sex alone provides an incomplete mechanical description. The findings identify anatomical and loading contrasts for experimental testing; their quantitative scope is passive static mechanics with fixed soft-tissue properties.
